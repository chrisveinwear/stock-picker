## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

Spark New Zealand is the country's largest integrated telecommunications provider, delivering mobile, broadband, voice, cloud computing, and IT managed services across New Zealand. The business model is straightforward: charge subscribers a monthly recurring fee for connectivity and related services, underpinned by network infrastructure — radio spectrum, cell towers, fibre access, and data centres — that costs hundreds of millions of dollars and years of regulatory process to replicate at scale.

Telecom economics sit comfortably inside the circle of competence. High fixed-cost networks create operating leverage; customer switching costs (bundled services, number portability friction, enterprise contract lengths) generate retention; and spectrum licensing provides a structural barrier to entry for a fourth operator. The competitive dynamics in a two-to-three-player NZ market mirror what Telstra has operated for decades in Australia — a structure this framework understands well.

The only segment requiring care is the enterprise IT and cloud division (Spark's "Digital" business), which competes against AWS, Microsoft Azure, and global system integrators. This sub-segment involves technology cycles and hyperscaler capital intensity that are harder to predict, but it represents a minority of Spark's revenue and does not change the fundamental nature of the underlying connectivity business.

**Status: Inside circle of competence. Analysis proceeds.**

### 2. Key Assumptions

The thesis depends on five explicit assumptions, each of which should be revisited at each quarterly check:

1. **The earnings trough was FY25, not FY26.** H1 FY26 EBITDAI rose 10.3% to NZD 448m — the first meaningful growth in several years. The bull case requires this not to be a one-time improvement. If H2 FY26 EBITDAI stalls or declines, the recovery thesis fails and the dividend becomes untenable.

2. **FCF recovers to NZD 320–400m by FY27.** Annual dividends at the implied NZD ~0.195/share rate cost approximately NZD 369m per year in aggregate (1,890.1m shares). FY25 FCF was only NZD 240m — a shortfall of ~NZD 129m. The thesis requires capex to stabilise at NZD 380–440m (down from the NZD 590m peak in FY24) and EBITDA to rise, delivering FCF coverage of at least 80–90%.

3. **Debt is refinanceable at manageable rates.** Total debt is NZD 2,349m, with net debt of NZD 2,315m. The declining RBNZ rate environment (OCR from ~5.5% to an estimated ~3.5% through 2025–26) is constructive. If the RBNZ reverses and rates rise 200bp, annual interest expense increases by approximately NZD 46m — a material hit to already-thin EBIT coverage.

4. **Revenue stabilises in the NZD 3,550–3,650m range.** Revenue has declined from NZD 3,875m (FY23) to NZD 3,623m (FY25) — a compounded −0.6% per annum decline, partly attributable to the Connexa tower monetisation normalising reported revenue. Stabilisation requires enterprise cloud revenue growth to offset ongoing consumer ARPU headwinds.

5. **NZD/AUD remains near 0.87.** Spark earns entirely in NZD; ASX investors receive dividends and earnings translated to AUD. A 5% NZD depreciation reduces the effective AUD yield from 11.1% to approximately 10.6% and compresses the AUD value of any capital appreciation.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Infrastructure (network assets), switching costs (bundled household and enterprise services), and limited scale advantages in a small domestic market.

**Morningstar's independent rating: Narrow Moat.** We concur.

Spark owns and operates New Zealand's largest mobile network and holds the largest market share in both mobile (~40–42% estimated) and residential broadband (~43% estimated). Its network investment and spectrum holdings took decades to accumulate. New entrants face a regulatory and capital hurdle that effectively prevents a credible fourth mobile operator from emerging in New Zealand. This constitutes a real infrastructure moat.

However, the moat is narrow rather than wide for several reasons:

- **Duopoly competition is genuine.** One NZ (formerly Vodafone NZ, now owned by Macquarie Infrastructure) is a well-capitalised rival with comparable 5G network coverage. Competition on mobile ARPU and enterprise contracts is active.
- **2degrees has expanded credibly.** The third operator has grown its 5G footprint and competes aggressively on mobile pricing, gaining share from both Spark and One NZ, particularly in prepaid and lower ARPU segments.
- **Fibre is regulated, not proprietary.** Spark's fixed-line broadband relies substantially on wholesale access to the Chorus UFB (Ultra-Fast Broadband) network. Spark cannot exclude rivals from the fibre infrastructure, limiting its broadband pricing power.
- **Enterprise cloud faces hyperscaler competition.** Spark's IT and cloud services business competes against AWS, Microsoft, and Google — companies with structural cost and innovation advantages that no domestic telco can match over a 10-year horizon.

**Five-year moat durability test:** Stable to slightly narrowing. The mobile spectrum moat is durable; the enterprise IT differentiation is less so. Spark's ability to bundle mobile, broadband, and managed services for SME and enterprise customers provides some stickiness, but the moat is being steadily tested by well-funded competitors investing in 5G infrastructure and cloud capabilities.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity:** No red flags. Spark's management team has operated underdual NZX/ASX disclosure obligations that require timely and accurate reporting. The August 2025 communication — "one of the most challenging periods in Spark's history" — was candid rather than euphemistic. No governance controversies, related-party transactions, or accounting irregularities appear in the available data.

**Capital allocation track record:** Mixed. The Connexa cell-tower monetisation (FY23, generating a reported NZD 1,135m net income figure inflated by asset-sale gains) was a reasonable move to surface latent infrastructure value. The proceeds were partially redeployed into network capex: NZD 590m in FY24, the highest in the observable period, causing FCF to collapse to NZD 174m and net debt to spike from NZD 1,730m to NZD 2,302m. Management has since moderated capex to NZD 440m (FY25), allowing FCF to recover to NZD 240m. The pattern suggests a management team willing to invest aggressively through a cycle — creditable in principle but painful for shareholders in execution.

**Owner mentality:** Moderate. The commitment to sustaining a high dividend through a trough earnings period signals shareholder orientation but edges toward capital-discipline risk if the payout is not covered by cash flows. A management team with true owner mentality would have cut the dividend earlier and reinvested at high returns; instead, Spark has maintained payouts that exceed FCF, suggesting some institutional imperative at work.

**Institutional imperative warning:** Telecom companies are expected by their investor base to pay high, stable dividends. Spark's management appears constrained by this expectation even when cash generation does not support it. The FY26 results on 20 August 2026 will be the defining test: does management right-size the dividend to match FCF reality, or does it continue borrowing to pay distributions?

### 13. Bridgewater — Risk Assessment

**Interest rate sensitivity: HIGH**

Spark's NZD 2,349m total debt is materially sensitive to the RBNZ rate cycle. The RBNZ cut its Official Cash Rate from approximately 5.5% to an estimated 3.5% through 2025, which reduces the refinancing cost on Spark's floating-rate debt tranches. Each 50bp RBNZ cut saves approximately NZD 12m in annual interest on NZD 2.35bn of debt — meaningful against FY25 net income of NZD 260m. The current rate-cut cycle is a genuine tailwind. A reversal — triggered by NZ inflation re-acceleration — would be a material headwind. Rate sensitivity at the current leverage level is asymmetric: cuts help marginally; hikes hurt significantly.

**Inflation sensitivity: MODERATE**

Telecom and IT services have partial inflation pass-through. Consumer broadband and mobile contracts can be repriced annually but competition limits how aggressively this can be done. Enterprise managed services contracts often carry CPI escalation clauses. Wage inflation is the primary opex sensitivity — Spark's workforce is skilled and relatively inelastic on headcount. Net inflation sensitivity: modest positive pass-through in a moderate inflation environment; net negative in a stagflation scenario where wages rise but volumes contract.

**Recession stress test:**

In a severe NZ recession (GDP −3 to −5%):
- Consumer mobile/broadband churn rises modestly (essential service); enterprise IT spend defers discretionary upgrades
- Estimated revenue decline: −10 to −15% (vs the −30% scenario modelled in Section 5)
- Estimated EBITDA at −12%: NZD 739m; EBITDA interest coverage: 739m / 139m = 5.3× — manageable
- Estimated EBIT at −25%: NZD 206m; EBIT interest coverage: 206m / 139m = 1.48× — thin, covenant-risk adjacent
- **Estimated drawdown: −35 to −50%** from current price (AUD 0.77–1.00) if recession triggers dividend cut and covenant concerns simultaneously

**Liquidity risk: LOW to MODERATE**

Cash on hand of NZD 34m is the lowest in the four-year history. Spark maintains access to revolving credit facilities with NZ and Australian banks, and its investment-grade credit rating (assumed, consistent with its debt market access history) provides refinancing capacity. However, any ratings downgrade would increase facility margins and reduce flexibility. Liquidity is adequate under normal conditions; it is a risk in a severe scenario.

**Leverage risk: HIGH**

Net debt / EBITDA = NZD 2,315m / NZD 840m = **2.75×**. Industry-typical comfort range is 2.0–2.5×; Spark is at the top of this range. Any EBITDA deterioration pushes toward 3.0× and potential covenant triggers. This is the single most important balance-sheet risk.
