## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

---

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Status: Inside — with caveats on technology evolution.**

Spark New Zealand is NZ's largest integrated telecommunications company (formerly Telecom New Zealand, rebranded 2014). It provides mobile connectivity, broadband, cloud and IT services, and entertainment (Spark Sport) exclusively within New Zealand. The business model is straightforward: acquire spectrum and build or lease network infrastructure, then sell access to consumers and enterprises on contract or prepay terms.

Telecom is among the most analysable of capital-intensive industries. Revenue is largely recurring, competitive dynamics in NZ are stable (effective duopoly of Spark and One NZ, with 2degrees a smaller third player), regulation is predictable under the Commerce Commission framework, and the product — connectivity — is non-discretionary. Spark divested its network infrastructure (Chorus) in 2011 so it is now a retail services company that pays Chorus wholesale access fees for fixed-line, reducing complexity.

The one caveat: 5G technology evolution creates a persistent capex obligation that is difficult to forecast precisely, and cloud/IT services ("Spark Health", enterprise IT) introduce competitive dimensions I understand less clearly than the core mobile business. Overall verdict: **inside the circle**, with full analytical weight on mobile/broadband and moderate scepticism about IT services margins.

---

### 2. Key Assumptions (Thesis Depends On These)

1. **Dividend is maintained near current levels.** At 10.8% yield, the stock's total return thesis depends critically on continued dividends. At NZD ~349m annual distribution vs NZD 360m FCF, the payout is at ~97% FCF cover — sustainable only if capex normalises as the 5G buildout peaks.

2. **5G capex cycle peaks in FY26–27 and FCF recovers to NZD 420m+ by FY28.** Capex of ~NZD 466m (est.) in FY26 is elevated. If growth capex normalises to NZD 380–400m, FCF expands materially and dividend cover improves to 1.1–1.2x.

3. **NZ mobile market remains a stable oligopoly.** Spark holds ~40% mobile revenue share. The thesis fails if a well-capitalised new entrant (e.g., a technology company) disrupts pricing, or if a merger between One NZ and 2degrees creates a stronger price-competitive rival.

4. **No adverse regulatory intervention on access pricing.** The Commerce Commission periodically reviews wholesale rates. A material reduction in mobile termination rates or retail price caps would compress margins.

5. **AUD/NZD rate remains broadly stable near 0.91.** Spark earns in NZD; ASX investors receive dividends and capital returns in NZD converted to AUD. A strengthening AUD erodes returns for Australian holders even if the underlying NZD business performs well.

---

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Switching costs + scale efficiency
**Moat strength:** Narrow
**Trend:** Stable

Spark's moat rests on two foundations. First, **switching costs**: mobile customers embedded in multi-product bundles (mobile + broadband + Spark Sport + business IT) face genuine friction to leave — number porting, contract exit fees, device financing, and bundle discounts all create inertia. Enterprise and SME customers in particular are sticky once cloud and IT services are integrated with mobile. Second, **scale in a small market**: NZ has a population of ~5.2m. Running a national mobile network at scale requires enormous fixed investment (spectrum, towers, core network) that cannot be economically replicated by a fourth entrant without a decade-long payback. This creates a structural barrier that limits competition.

However, the moat is not wide because:
- Spark has no pricing power beyond CPI; attempts to push ARPU materially above One NZ invite churn.
- Gross margins of 31.7% and operating margins of 7.6% are thin for a business with genuine switching costs — a wide-moat business (think Visa or CSL) would show 40%+ operating margins.
- Technology disruption is real: if fixed-wireless access from low-earth-orbit satellite (Starlink) erodes Spark's broadband base, the bundling economics weaken.
- ROIC estimated at ~5.6% (NOPAT NZD 202m / invested capital NZD 3,585m) is below most hurdle rates, suggesting the competitive position does not generate truly exceptional returns on capital.

The moat is genuine but narrow, protecting the business from aggressive share loss rather than generating outsized returns. **Five-year durability: stable** — no new entrant can credibly build a competing NZ mobile network in that timeframe, but pricing power will remain compressed.

### 4. Management Assessment

**CEO:** Jolie Hodson (appointed September 2020). **CFO:** Stefan Knight.

**Integrity check (auto-veto threshold):** No governance red flags identified. No related-party transaction concerns, no restatements, no regulatory sanctions.

**Capital allocation track record:**
Hodson inherited a business under revenue pressure from fixed-line decline and set a clear strategy: simplify the portfolio, invest in 5G, grow cloud and IT services, and return cash to shareholders via a high dividend. The divestment of non-core assets (including the IT services reselling business) and renegotiation of Chorus access agreements demonstrate disciplined focus. The 5G investment commitment is strategically correct for a national operator but puts pressure on near-term FCF. The decision to maintain a very high dividend payout ratio despite elevated capex is the key capital allocation concern — it implies either confidence that capex will fall, or a reluctance to cut the dividend even if that would be the more prudent path.

**Owner mentality:** Management own a modest quantum of shares. No material insider buying at current depressed prices, which is a mild negative signal. The institutional imperative is present: dividend guidance has been communicated to the market and a cut would be painful, potentially causing management to sustain it beyond prudent FCF levels.

**Assessment:** Competent and straightforward, but the dividend policy is the central management risk. The business is not poorly run; the tension is between strategic investment requirements and a distribution commitment that leaves little financial flexibility.

#### Valuation Reconciliation

The code model midpoint of AUD 1.93 diverges from (a) analyst consensus AUD 2.85 (+48%) and (b) Morningstar implied fair value AUD 2.94 (+52%). Both divergences exceed the 20% threshold, requiring explicit reconciliation.

**Step 1 — Check authoritative inputs for error.** Revenue NZD 3.70bn, EBITDA NZD 840m, FCF NZD 360m, net debt NZD 2.23bn, shares 1,890.1m, AUD/NZD 0.91 — all confirmed cross-consistent with the authoritative data block. EPS trailing AUD 0.12 × 1,890.1m = AUD 226.8m; at 0.91 rate = NZD 249.2m, consistent with estimated net income. No hallucinated inputs found.

**Step 2 — Identify the assumption driving the gap.** The single dominant driver is the **8% WACC policy floor**. Spark's beta of 0.13 implies a true CAPM cost of equity of 4.3% + (0.13 × 5.5%) = approximately 5.0%. With a capital structure roughly 60% debt / 40% equity and after-tax cost of debt of ~4.3% (6% × 0.72), the theoretically correct WACC is approximately 4.6–5.2%. The policy floor at 8.0% is effectively 1.6–3.4 percentage points above the market-implied discount rate for this stock. This creates a large mechanical downward bias in the DCF.

**Step 3 — Adjusted assumptions.** If WACC is relaxed to 6.5% (a level consistent with Morningstar's likely assumption for a narrow-moat, low-beta utility-adjacent business), the DCF produces approximately AUD 2.40–2.60 (I computed AUD 2.56 under 6.5% WACC, 2.5% terminal growth, 7× exit multiple in the Morgan Stanley section). This is materially closer to the analyst consensus of AUD 2.85 and Morningstar's AUD 2.94; the residual gap of ~10–13% is explained by analysts using slightly higher normalised earnings or a more optimistic capex normalisation scenario.

**Step 4 — Reconciled view.** If the 8% floor policy is taken as binding (appropriate for high-debt companies where a margin of safety on the discount rate is warranted), then AUD 1.93 is correct under that constraint. However, the 8% floor on a 0.13-beta stock applies a penalty that arguably over-corrects. My reconciled view is that **intrinsic value is AUD 1.52–2.57 under policy constraints, but the economic fair value is more likely AUD 2.20–2.60** if the discount rate better reflects Spark's true risk. I maintain the policy IV range in the frontmatter but acknowledge this undervalues the stock relative to both Morningstar and the sell-side consensus.

**Step 5 — Model warning.** The code flags that IV swings 54% across the sensitivity grid, confirming high sensitivity to WACC and terminal growth. This is characteristic of capital-intensive businesses with large terminal values. Widen margin of safety accordingly — 30% minimum to midpoint IV, 30% to AUD 1.35.

**Morningstar cross-check:** Morningstar assigns a Narrow moat (consistent with my assessment) and a fair value implying AUD 2.94, at which Spark trades at a 47% discount. Morningstar's higher FV is consistent with their likely use of a lower discount rate (~6%) and a more optimistic FCF recovery profile post-5G. I do not blend Morningstar's FV into my IV arithmetic, but treat it as an independent validation that the stock is meaningfully undervalued versus a reasonable assumption set.

### 17. McKinsey Macro Context (Australia/New Zealand)

**RBNZ Rate Outlook and Impact:**
The Reserve Bank of New Zealand has been in an easing cycle through 2025–26, with the OCR having been cut from 5.5% (peak) to approximately 3.0–3.25% as of mid-2026. Further cuts of 25–50bps are expected over the next 12 months. This is the most important macro tailwind for Spark: lower rates (a) reduce the company's debt refinancing cost on rolling NZD 2.32bn of debt, (b) narrow the spread that makes Spark's 10.8% yield look "too good to be true," and (c) encourage institutional rotation into yield-generating defensives. Each 50bps RBNZ cut historically re-rates NZ/ASX telcos 8–12% on a forward P/E basis.

**NZ Economic Conditions:**
New Zealand GDP growth is subdued (~1.5–2.0% forecast for 2026), with the economy recovering from a technical recession in 2023–24. Consumer confidence is improving but household balance sheets remain stretched from prior high rates. This low-growth environment is mildly negative for enterprise IT spending (Spark Business growth) but supports the defensive nature of mobile/broadband demand (non-discretionary).

**Australian Economic Context (ASX investor perspective):**
The RBA has also been easing (OCR likely ~3.5–3.75% by mid-2026). An improving Australian yield environment reduces the relative attractiveness of high-yield ASX equities to some degree (term deposits above 5% competed with yield stocks in 2023–24), but as cash rates fall toward 3%, Spark's 10.8% yield looks increasingly compelling to Australian income investors who own it cross-listed.

**AUD/NZD:**
Currently ~0.91 AUD per NZD. Both economies are in easing cycles, limiting large divergence. A weaker NZD (e.g., 0.85) would reduce AUD-equivalent dividends by ~7%; a stronger NZD (e.g., 0.95) would boost them. The pair is unlikely to move more than ±5% in a 12-month base case. Net: FX is a modest risk, not a dominant one.

**Sector rotation signals:**
As both central banks cut rates, global capital has rotated from cash to bonds to yield equities. Within the ASX, this rotation has begun lifting REITs, infrastructure, and utilities; telcos are a natural next beneficiary. Spark, with the highest yield on the ASX/NZX telco sector, is a prime re-rating candidate in this rotation.
