## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside the circle.** Domino's Pizza Enterprises (DMP) is the master franchisor for Domino's Pizza across Australia/New Zealand, Japan, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Germany, Denmark, Luxembourg, Malaysia, and Singapore — approximately 3,800 stores across those territories. It is not the US parent (Domino's Pizza LLC); it licenses the brand for its markets.

The business model is simple to describe: DMP grants franchisees the right to operate Domino's stores in exchange for royalties (a percentage of system sales), sells them food, packaging, and commissary services at a margin, and in some markets operates company-owned stores. Revenue is thus a blend of commissary/supply income, royalties, and owned-store revenues.

Consumer franchise businesses with a recognised brand, recurring royalty income, a centralised supply chain, and a defined unit economics model sit firmly within the circle of competence. The complexity in DMP's case is not the model itself — it is multi-country execution risk and whether the Japan and European markets can recover from a costly over-expansion cycle. That operational complexity is legible; the business itself is not opaque.

---

### 2. Key Assumptions

The entire thesis rests on five explicit assumptions that must be verified each half:

1. **Operating margin normalises toward 5–5.5% net margin by FY28.** TTM net margin is 2.7% (including the effective first annual loss year in FY25). Forward consensus EPS of AUD 1.35 embeds partial recovery. Full recovery to historical norms (~7–8% net) is the Morningstar bull case; partial recovery is the base.

2. **The EDLP strategy pivot succeeds.** Announced in August 2025, DMP is abandoning discounting and vouchers in favour of everyday-low-prices (EDLP) modelled on Bunnings. The February 2026 H1 result still disappointed, and the April 2026 trading update sent shares down another 10%, indicating the pivot has not yet stabilised same-store sales. Evidence of traction needed within the next two halves.

3. **Japan does not require further material write-downs.** Japan is the single largest drag. The assumption is that the store rationalisation announced in FY25 is substantially complete, and Japan EBITDA reaches breakeven by H2 FY27. A further impairment wave would materially change this thesis.

4. **Debt remains serviceable through the turnaround.** Net debt of AUD 1.17bn against EBITDA of AUD 200M (5.85× leverage) leaves thin covenant headroom. The thesis assumes FCF of ~AUD 120M p.a. is directed toward debt reduction and no earnings deterioration forces a refinancing at punitive rates.

5. **The master franchise agreement with Domino's Pizza LLC (US) is not renegotiated on worse terms.** DMP's entire franchise value is contingent on this licence. Any forced renegotiation — royalty uplift, territory surrender, or non-renewal — would constitute a fundamental threat to the business model.

---

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Intangible brand + supply chain scale. **Morningstar rating: Narrow — agreed.**

**Moat strength: Narrow.** Domino's is among the world's most recognised pizza delivery brands. DMP's franchisees benefit from brand recognition built over decades by the US parent. The centralised commissary model creates real procurement cost advantages that an individual franchisee cannot replicate. The proprietary technology platform (app, GPS tracking, digital ordering) generates modest switching costs and operational leverage across thousands of stores.

**Moat trend: Narrowing.** Three structural forces are eroding the sources of advantage:

- **Platform aggregators (Uber Eats, DoorDash)** have commoditised last-mile delivery infrastructure, removing DMP's decisive technology advantage and allowing local competitors to participate in delivery without a Domino's-like capex commitment. The delivery moat that justified the 2021 premium valuation has largely been competed away.
- **Sustained consumer value-sensitivity** has forced the EDLP strategy — a structurally lower-margin response to competition. Pricing power, historically a hallmark of a wide-moat franchise, is absent.
- **Store saturation.** DMP accelerated store openings in Japan and Europe at return-dilutive economics, cannibalising same-store sales and burdening franchisees. The subsequent rationalisation destroys capital and signals the prior expansion was value-destructive.

**Five-year moat durability test (to 2031):** The Domino's brand will persist. Commissary scale economics will persist. But the structural shift in third-party delivery and the permanent reset of pricing power mean the moat in 2031 will be narrower than in 2021. An investor buying today is not buying the 2021 franchise — they are buying the post-rationalisation franchise, which is smaller, more indebted, and competing in a commoditised delivery landscape.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity: Pass.** No governance red flags with the current board and the CEO appointed in 2023. Management has been transparent about inherited operational failures.

**Capital allocation track record (prior regime): Poor.** Don Meij's tenure produced one of the more egregious examples of institutional imperative on the ASX. The Japan acquisition (2013), European accelerated roll-out, and the store-count growth story justified to the market at multiples exceeding AUD 150 per share consumed enormous capital. Return on invested capital has now fallen below the cost of capital, confirming the expansion was serially value-destructive at the terminal margins achieved.

**New management (Mark van Dyck, CEO from 2023):** Directionally correct. The EDLP pivot, store rationalisation, and operational cost focus are the right medicine. The question is whether execution follows intent. The new CEO has not yet been tested through a full recovery cycle. He has not signalled share repurchases or meaningful insider buying, which would provide conviction.

**Institutional imperative warning:** The temptation under new management will be to re-accelerate store openings before the base is stabilised, under pressure from franchisees seeking growth. Any premature re-acceleration would repeat the Meij-era mistake. Watch capital allocation decisions closely at FY26 results.

### 13. Bridgewater Risk Assessment

Conversely, the RBA cutting cycle (expected through FY26–27) is a meaningful tailwind, potentially saving AUD 24–36M annually on a 200–300bp cut cycle.

**Inflation sensitivity:** MODERATELY NEGATIVE. Food and labour costs are DMP's two largest input cost categories at the franchisee level. Persistent above-trend inflation compresses franchisee margins, leading to store closures or reduced franchisee investment — both negative for DMP's royalty base.

**Recession stress test:** In a mild recession (GDP −1% to −2%), consumer trade-down from casual dining to QSR is historically positive for pizza delivery. However, DMP's leverage means a simultaneous earnings decline and rate spike would be dangerous. Estimated recession drawdown from current levels: **−30 to −35%** (to AUD 10–11). In a severe recession with covenant breach scenario: **−50%+ potential** (to AUD 7–8).

**Liquidity risk:** Cash AUD 70M, FCF AUD 120M p.a. — adequate for normal operations. The risk is a renegotiation or refinancing event. If AUD 1.24bn in debt comes up for refinancing in a high-rate environment with below-covenant EBITDA, the business could face a dilutive equity raise.

**Leverage risk:** At 5.85× EV/EBITDA net leverage, DMP sits in the private equity / LBO zone — not a quality investment firm's comfort zone. Investment-grade franchise operators typically run 2–3× leverage.

**Tail risk scenarios:**
