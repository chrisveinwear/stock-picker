## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside.** Endeavour Group is a simple business to explain: it owns Australia's two largest liquor retail banners (Dan Murphy's — big-box, price-leadership format; BWS — convenience-format, historically co-located with Woolworths supermarkets) plus ALH Group, the country's largest pub and gaming-machine operator. Revenue is earned from (a) retail markup on beverage alcohol sold at scale, and (b) food, beverage and gaming revenue from ~300 venues, a meaningful share of which comes from poker machines operating under state-issued licences. No exotic financial engineering, no complex global supply chain, no technology platform risk. This sits comfortably in the consumer-staples/retail sleeve of my circle of competence.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

1. ALH's poker-machine revenue does not suffer severe structural impairment from cashless-gaming-card / harm-minimisation reforms in Victoria and NSW over the explicit forecast horizon.
2. Dan Murphy's and BWS retain price and scale leadership against Coles Group's Liquorland/Vintage Cellars banners and independents, preventing further gross-margin erosion below the current 34.9%.
3. Management continues to prioritise balance-sheet repair — the FY24→FY25 total-debt reduction (AUD 6,072m → 5,817m) and capex discipline (capex −20.3% YoY) — over debt-funded growth or M&A.
4. Australian per-capita alcohol consumption stabilises rather than extending its multi-year secular decline (health/moderation trends among younger cohorts).
5. Refinancing costs on ~AUD 5.35bn of debt remain manageable through the current rate cycle — not independently verified against a supplied RBA rate path; treated as an assumption, not a data point.

If (1) breaks — a genuine, not hypothetical, legislative risk — the earnings base this valuation rests on is too high.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** hybrid — (a) scale/cost advantage in retail liquor distribution (purchasing scale, store density, Dan Murphy's "lowest liquor price guarantee" positioning), and (b) a regulatory/licence-based moat in ALH's gaming operations (poker-machine licences are capped in number and geographically entrenched, genuinely hard to replicate).

**Strength: narrow-to-moderate.** I'm not prepared to underwrite Morningstar's "Wide" rating here (see reconciliation in Section 6). Retail liquor at the BWS/Dan Murphy's level is a low-differentiation, price-competitive category — the moat is real but thin, resting on scale rather than pricing power. The gaming licence moat is more genuine but is a regulatory asset, not an economic one, and regulatory assets can be taken away by legislators, not competitors.

**Trend: narrowing.** FY25 revenue fell -2.0% YoY (AUD 12,309m → 12,058m) and net income fell -16.8% YoY (AUD 512m → 426m) against a FY22–25 revenue CAGR of only ~1.3%. Return on equity has declined from 14.3% (FY23) to an implied 11.1% (FY25, net income/equity) to the current trailing 9.4%. That is a moat under pressure, not one widening.

**Five-year durability test:** the retail liquor scale advantage is likely durable — Dan Murphy's and BWS should still be the largest players in five years. The gaming licence moat is the genuine unresolved question: cashless gaming card mandates, if extended and enforced as currently being trialled in Victoria and NSW, could structurally impair pokie revenue economics industry-wide. This is the single biggest swing factor in the thesis and belongs at the top of the monitoring list.
