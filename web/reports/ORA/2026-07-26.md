## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside, with care.** Orora is a rigid packaging manufacturer — glass bottles (predominantly for the Australian wine and spirits industry, made in a vertically integrated furnace at Gawler, South Australia), aluminium beverage cans, and a smaller Visual Communications (point-of-sale/display) business. This is a simple business to explain: it makes the container the product ships in, sells on multi-year supply contracts to beverage and wine producers, and earns a margin on glass and metal conversion. It sits squarely in "boring manufacturing" — the kind of business Buffett has owned for decades in the form of packaging and industrial names.

The one thing that requires care: Orora divested its larger, lower-margin North American packaging distribution business (visible in the financial history below as the revenue collapse from AUD 4,698m in FY2024 to AUD 2,090m in FY2025, alongside a large one-off net income print of AUD 973m). The company today is materially smaller and more concentrated than its FY2022–24 financial history suggests. That's a business-model change, not just a bad year, and it means older multi-year averages should be read with that discontinuity in mind.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

1. The post-divestiture entity (Australasian Glass, Cans, Visual Communications) can sustain or rebuild operating margins now that the lower-margin NA distribution drag is gone.
2. The Gawler glass furnace and can-manufacturing scale continue to function as a real (if narrow) cost/logistics moat — glass is expensive to freight, favouring domestic incumbents.
3. The FY2025 balance-sheet transformation (total debt down from AUD 2,418m to AUD 653m; cash up to AUD 257m; equity up to AUD 2,897m) is durable and not reversed by a near-term releveraging M&A move.
4. The ~6.9% dividend yield is maintained near current levels without a cut, even though trailing EPS (AUD 0.10) implies a payout ratio close to fully distributed.
5. True normalised earnings power sits closer to the historical owner-earnings/FCF-based estimates (~AUD 116–171m) than to the optimistic forward-EPS read, given FY2025's numbers are distorted by transition/one-off items.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

- **Moat type:** Cost/scale (vertically integrated glass manufacturing, logistics-driven — glass is heavy and costly to ship, favouring a domestic incumbent) plus modest switching costs (co-designed bottle specifications and multi-year wine/spirits supply contracts). Visual Communications is a weaker, more competitive segment with limited differentiation.
- **Strength:** Narrow. This matches the valuation model's own quality-tier classification (narrow, 10x owner-earnings multiple, 10x terminal exit multiple). Beverage cans are closer to a commodity-manufacturing business with several credible competitors (Ball, Crown, Orora, Visy/CCL) than a wide-moat franchise.
- **Trend:** Arguably **widening for the core, but off a smaller base.** Divesting the lower-return North American distribution business should, in principle, lift blended ROIC and simplify the story — but that is a thesis to be proven in the next two or three half-year reports, not yet demonstrated in the trailing numbers (ROE 4.4%, net margin 5.7% are still weak for a business claiming even a narrow moat).
- **Five-year durability test:** Would Orora's glass and cans business still exist largely unchanged in 2031? Likely yes — Australian wine and spirits producers aren't relocating their bottling, and can demand is structurally stable. But margin durability (can the business earn attractive returns on the capital now recommitted to the smaller footprint) is the open question, not existential survival.
