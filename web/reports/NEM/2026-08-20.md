## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Outside — with a narrow exception for "lowest-cost producer" analysis.** Newmont is the world's largest gold producer by volume (approx. 5.3 Moz in 2025), operating across nine countries. The business model is simple: dig ore, process it, sell gold at the prevailing global price. But simplicity ≠ understandability in the Buffett sense. Gold mining is a **price-taker commodity business** — Newmont has zero pricing power. Its revenues are a function of (volume × gold price) minus costs it cannot fully control (labour, energy, regulation, ore grade decline). The only durable competitive advantage in this industry is being the **lowest-cost operator with the longest reserve life** — a "cost moat" that survives gold-price cycles. Morningstar explicitly rates Newmont's economic moat as **None**. Unless we can verify Newmont sits sustainably in the bottom quartile of the global cost curve *and* has reserve growth exceeding depletion, this sits outside the circle of competence for a buy-and-hold value investor. We analyse it here only to demonstrate why it fails the moat test.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

The thesis rests on these explicit, verifiable assumptions:

1. **Gold price mean-reversion risk**: Spot gold ~AUD 4,363/oz (USD ~2,900/oz at 0.665 FX) is near all-time highs. Newmont's 2025 AISC was ~USD 1,100/oz (implied from 68% gross margin). A 30% gold price decline to USD 2,000/oz would cut EBITDA by ~50% and FCF by >70%. The market is pricing in sustained high gold prices.
2. **No structural moat**: Morningstar's "None" rating is correct. Scale brings some procurement/financing advantages, but not pricing power or switching costs. Reserve replacement is a treadmill, not a moat.
3. **Maintenance capex ≈ depreciation**: For a mature miner, sustaining capital (to maintain current production) runs ~USD 2.5–3.0bn/year. Owner earnings = net income + D&A − maintenance capex ≈ FCF. The code model's normalised owner earnings base of AUD 2.7bn aligns with this.
4. **AUD/USD = 0.665**: Derived from spot gold AUD 4,363 / USD 2,900. Used for all USD→AUD conversions. Sensitivity: ±5% FX moves IV by ~±AUD 3–4.
5. **Cost of equity floor 8% is appropriate**: The code model's 8% CAPM floor (risk-free 4.3% + beta 0.6 × ERP 5.5% = 7.6% → floored) is reasonable for a low-beta, commodity-levered equity. Using a lower rate would be wishful thinking.

| Dimension | Assessment | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Moat type** | None (commodity) | Price taker; no intangible, switching, network, or scale moat that survives gold-price downturns |
| **Moat strength** | None | Morningstar: "None". Peer cost curves show Newmont in 2nd–3rd quartile AISC, not 1st |
| **Moat trend** | Narrowing | Reserve depletion > replacement; grade decline at legacy assets (Carlin, Yanacocha); jurisdictional risk rising (Ghana, Peru, Canada) |
| **5-year durability test** | Fails | At USD 1,800/oz gold, ~30% of industry is unprofitable. Newmont survives but FCF → 0. No pricing power to offset. |

**Scale advantages** (procurement, financing, technical expertise) are real but **competed away** in gold mining — they lower costs marginally but don't create barriers to entry. The product is fungible; customers (refiners, central banks) don't care who mined it. **Switching costs = zero.** **Intangible assets** (brand, permits) are necessary but not sufficient — permits are a cost of entry, not a moat. **Network effects = none.**

**Conclusion:** This is a **cyclical commodity equity**, not a compounding franchise. Buffett's 2020 Barrick purchase (quickly sold) was a macro hedge, not a business investment. The same logic applies here.
