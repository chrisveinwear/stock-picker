## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

Inside the boundary, but not comfortably so. Collins Foods is Australia's largest KFC franchisee (and operator of Taco Bell in Australia, plus a growing KFC franchise footprint in the Netherlands and now Germany). The business model is simple to state: lease or build restaurants, operate them under a franchise agreement with Yum! Brands, collect the spread between store-level cash flow and rent/labor/franchise fees. That's easy to explain to a ten-year-old.

What pushes it toward the boundary rather than the center of the circle is that CKF doesn't own the brand it depends on — Yum! sets menu strategy, franchise fee structure, and territory rights, and can in principle not renew or renegotiate terms unfavorably. This isn't outside the circle (the economics are legible and I can underwrite the downside), but it's not a "toll bridge" business either. CKF sits within the **consumer cyclical / restaurant** category, adjacent to but not squarely inside the "consumer staples" bucket this portfolio is built around — QSR spending flexes more with discretionary income than packaged groceries do.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

1. **KFC Australia same-store sales and margins hold up** even as the European segment (Netherlands + newly acquired Bavaria, Germany restaurants) drags on group profitability in the near term — per the FY26 result commentary, Europe is explicitly flagged as the weak spot.
2. **The German acquisition (8 restaurants, ~€31.1m, completed 1 June 2026) is accretive within 2–3 years**, not a value-destructive capital allocation decision made to chase growth outside the core Australian franchise.
3. **Leverage stays serviceable.** Total debt of ~AUD 843m against equity of ~AUD 422m (D/E ~2.0x) requires stable-to-growing EBITDA; the thesis assumes no material deterioration in Australian trading that would strain covenants.
4. **The Yum! Brands franchise relationship is stable** over the 10-year explicit forecast horizon — no adverse renegotiation of fee structure or territory rights.
5. **Statutory earnings volatility (FY23 NI 13m → FY24 77m → FY25 9m → FY26 44m) is largely one-off/impairment-driven**, and underlying earnings power is closer to the FY26 "record result" and the 52.0cps underlying EPS figure cited in recent coverage than to the noisy statutory numbers.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** intangible (brand association via franchise) + modest scale/location advantages in the Australian QSR real-estate network. **Strength: narrow, at best.** This assessment lines up with Morningstar's own **"None"** economic moat rating on this stock — a useful independent cross-check that I don't think is wrong. CKF doesn't set its own pricing umbrella independent of Yum! Brands strategy, faces direct competition from Guzman y Gomez, Domino's, McDonald's (privately operated in Australia) and a fragmented casual-dining/QSR field, and operates on a 51.2% gross margin that collapses to a 7.0% operating margin and just 2.8% net margin once rent, labor and corporate costs are absorbed — not the economics of a wide-moat compounder.

**Trend: stable to modestly narrowing.** The push into Germany (a new geography, new currency exposure, and — per the news flow — a "weak Europe outlook" flagged alongside the FY26 result) adds execution risk and capital intensity at a time the balance sheet is already levered near 2x equity. That's a moat-widening bet (more scale, more territory) funded in a moat-narrowing way (more debt, more geographic complexity).

**Five-year durability test:** the Australian KFC franchise almost certainly still exists and is still profitable in five years — the format is durable and cash-generative (FCF positive in all four years of provided history: 71m, 94m, 114m, 96m). What's far less certain is whether the *European* expansion is still part of the story on the same terms, or whether it gets written down, sold, or requires further capital. I would not describe this as a business with a moat that reliably widens on its own; it widens only if management executes disciplined store-level economics in a market (Europe) where CKF has comparatively little history.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity:** no red flags surfaced in the data provided. A recent item — Collins Foods correcting typographical errors in its 2026 AGM Notice of Meeting — is trivial and administrative, not a governance concern, but it's the kind of detail worth a quarterly glance to make sure nothing more substantive follows it.

**Capital allocation track record:** mixed-to-reasonable. On the credit side: capex has been trending down (76m → 83m → 68m → 54m across FY23–FY26) even as revenue grew, and the fully franked dividend was raised 7.7% alongside the FY26 result — a shareholder-friendly signal paid for out of genuinely strong free cash flow. On the debit side: the ~€31.1m Bavaria KFC acquisition adds debt-funded growth into a segment management itself is now flagging as weak, and total debt has been essentially flat-to-up (843m → 835m → 891m → 843m) rather than steadily paid down despite four straight years of positive FCF — cash generated is being recycled into expansion and dividends rather than deleveraging a balance sheet that's already stretched by this framework's standards (D/E 2.0x vs. the <1.0x screening threshold).

**Owner mentality / institutional imperative:** the German acquisition, announced with fanfare in March 2026 and completed in June, has the flavor of "growth for growth's sake" that Buffett warns about — expanding into a new, unproven geography while the market is simultaneously punishing the stock (down 2.75% on FY26 result day) for exactly that kind of guidance. I'd want to see the Bavaria stores prove out their unit economics before crediting management with more capital to deploy abroad.

**Valuation Reconciliation**

The model itself flags low confidence: a 40% swing in IV across the sensitivity grid, a beta (0.46) that had to be clamped up to the 0.60 floor because it understates the equity risk of a business carrying net debt at 77% of market cap, and a cost of equity floored at 8.0% (versus an unfloored 7.6%) that I still think is too low given that leverage. The ~2x gap between the full DCF (15.81) and the owner-earnings-multiple (7.94) is a terminal-value artifact: at an 8% discount rate and 2.5% terminal growth, the perpetuity-growth leg implies a terminal multiple near 18x, averaged against a 12.5x exit multiple — a mismatch that pulls the blended DCF well above what a normalized earnings multiple alone would support for a narrow/no-moat, 2.0x-levered restaurant franchisee.

My adjustment is to **assumption values, not model structure**: raising the effective cost of equity toward ~9% (reflecting the leverage the clamped beta likely still understates) and leaning more heavily on the owner-earnings-multiple and Graham anchors than on the unadjusted 10-year DCF, which I think overweights an optimistic near-term growth fade for a company that just told the market Europe is soft. That reweighting pulls my fair value toward the **AUD 7.20–11.00** range, midpoint **~9.10** — close to the code model's 7.94 (+14.6%, under the 20% mandatory-reconciliation trigger) and about 11% below the AUD 10.27 analyst consensus target, both comfortably inside the sanity bounds set for this report.

I read Morningstar's higher figure as likely assuming a fuller margin recovery and successful European integration than I'm willing to underwrite given the balance sheet and the "weak Europe outlook" management itself just flagged. I'm not dismissing their view — it's a legitimate independent data point — but I'm weighting my own leverage-adjusted, multiple-anchored valuation more heavily for the buy/sell thresholds below.
