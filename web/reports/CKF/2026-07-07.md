## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside — with one caveat at the boundary.**

Collins Foods is a quick-service restaurant (QSR) franchise operator. Its business model is among the most transparent in the consumer sector: Collins licences the KFC and Taco Bell brands from Yum! Brands, operates standardised restaurants, and earns revenue from food sales. Product innovation, marketing, and brand stewardship costs fall primarily on Yum! — Collins's job is to execute at scale and manage labour, food costs, and lease obligations efficiently.

The Australian KFC business is unambiguously inside my circle. I can model it: same-store sales growth is the primary revenue driver; margin is squeezed by labour (award wage inflation) and food input costs; network expansion produces linear new-unit economics; franchisor royalties (approximately 5–6% of revenue) are a fixed headwind.

The **European expansion** (Netherlands acquired ~2016; Bavaria Germany, 8 restaurants acquired and completed as of Q1 FY2026) sits at the outer boundary. Dutch and German consumer dynamics, competitive fast-food density, lease markets, and labour regulation differ materially from Australia. Per June 2026 FY results news, Collins itself acknowledges "Europe headwinds" and a "weak Europe outlook." I can analyse it, but with lower confidence, and will apply extra margin of safety accordingly.

**Verdict: Inside circle. Proceed.**

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Narrow (derivative scale + location network) · **Strength:** Narrow · **Trend:** Stable in Australia, uncertain in Europe

Collins Foods does not own the KFC brand — Yum! Brands does — and this is the central moat limitation. As a franchisee, Collins's competitive advantages are second-order:

**Scale in Australia:** Collins is the largest KFC franchisee in Australia, operating approximately 250+ stores. This scale creates genuine purchasing efficiencies (food and packaging procurement), shared back-office leverage, and better terms from suppliers relative to a smaller operator. A new entrant cannot replicate this network cheaply.

**Location portfolio:** CKF has secured freehold and leasehold positions in high-traffic Australian locations over decades. The physical site network is difficult and slow to replicate.

**Franchise agreement as a limited moat:** KFC franchise licences are long-term agreements. A competitor cannot open a KFC restaurant without Yum!'s blessing. This creates a degree of barrier to competition within CKF's geography.

**What limits the moat:** Collins pays royalties (estimated ~5–6% of revenue) to Yum!, constraining margin. Collins cannot materially alter the product, brand, or pricing strategy. If Yum! decides to favour a different franchisee in any region, or to open company-operated stores, Collins's advantage erodes. The competitive moat belongs to the brand owner, not the operator.

**Morningstar rates the moat as None.** I respectfully disagree at the margin — the Australian network scale and location advantage are real barriers — but I accept the spirit of Morningstar's view: Collins does not have a self-sustaining competitive advantage independent of the Yum! relationship. I rate the moat **narrow**, with a five-year durability assessment of **stable**: the Australian business is well-entrenched, the European business adds risk without clearly adding moat.

**Five-year moat durability:** Australian KFC brand remains strong; fast food is resilient in downturns (trading-down effect). No structural threat on the horizon. European uncertainty is a drag. Overall durability: stable-to-slight-erosion due to European execution risk.

1. **Consumer spending:** Lower mortgage rates reduce household stress, lifting discretionary restaurant spend. KFC's value-segment positioning means it benefits from trading-down (from full-service restaurants) and from improving consumer confidence.
2. **Balance sheet:** CKF's floating-rate debt (portion of AUD 843m) costs decline with rate cuts. Each 25bps RBA cut reduces annual interest costs by ~AUD 2m (approximate, assuming ~40% floating exposure).
3. **Property lease costs:** Commercial property yields are rate-sensitive; lower rates over time reduce new lease negotiation pressures.

**Australian inflation and GDP trends:**

Inflation has moderated from the FY2023 peak that compressed CKF margins. With food and labour inflation returning toward trend, the conditions that triggered FY2023 and FY2025 net income troughs are reversing. Australian GDP growth is modest but positive — not recession territory, supportive of the consumer recovery thesis.

CKF has meaningful EUR exposure through its Netherlands and German operations. A strengthening AUD (which would follow a global risk-on environment) would translate European EUR earnings at a less favourable rate. However, given that European operations are currently loss-making, AUD strength actually *reduces* the translated loss burden. If and when European operations turn profitable, this becomes a negative translation factor.

**Sector rotation signals:**

Consumer discretionary stocks (CKF's sector) typically underperform during rate-tightening cycles and re-rate when cuts begin. The current environment — early-to-mid rate-cut cycle — is historically favourable for consumer discretionary re-ratings. Institutional investors rotating from defensive sectors (e.g., utilities, healthcare) toward consumer discretionary benefit CKF.
