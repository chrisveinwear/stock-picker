## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside — straightforward business model, well within circle.**

Brambles' core business is CHEP: it owns a global pool of standardised wooden pallets (plus plastic crates and containers) and rents them to manufacturers who place goods on them, ship them through the supply chain to retailers, and then return them to CHEP collection points for repair and re-issue. The unit economics are intuitive — Brambles earns a net pooling fee per pallet per trip, and every additional pallet in the network lowers the unit economics for potential competitors. There is no technological black box, no complex financial engineering, and no regulatory regime that is difficult to decode. Revenue visibility is high because CHEP operates under long-term supply agreements with blue-chip FMCG and grocery customers. Capex intensity (pallets wear out and must be replaced) is real and measurable. The business can be explained to a ten-year-old in two sentences: Brambles owns the wooden boards your groceries sit on, and it charges companies to use them. This is firmly inside the circle.

---

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5 — thesis-level)

The following assumptions underpin the investment thesis. Each should be monitored quarterly.

1. **CHEP's pooling network maintains structural pricing power.** Brambles contracts are typically multi-year and volume-linked, with price escalation clauses tied to CPI and wood/labour cost pass-throughs. If CHEP's pricing power erodes (e.g., customers build proprietary pallet pools or new entrants achieve competitive density), the moat weakens and the valuation must be revised down.

2. **Revenue growth re-accelerates from 2–3% (FY26 guidance) back toward 4–5% in FY27–28.** The May 2026 guidance downgrade reflected softer consumer spending volumes in the Americas (CHEP's largest market). The thesis assumes this is cyclical, not structural — that volume recovery follows macroeconomic normalisation. If the volume weakness is structural (permanent share loss to in-house pallet management), stage-1 growth must be cut, reducing the DCF meaningfully.

3. **Operating margins expand toward 24–25% over the medium term.** H1 2026 results showed margin improvement, and digitalisation of pallet tracking (IoT/RFID) is expected to reduce shrinkage and empty-trip costs. Failure to achieve operating leverage (e.g., inflationary input cost pressure outpacing pricing) would compress owner earnings.

4. **The US$400m buyback is completed at prices that enhance per-share value.** At current prices near intrinsic value, this is roughly value-neutral. If management executes the buyback above intrinsic value, it destroys capital.

5. **AUD/USD exchange rate remains broadly stable around 0.640.** Brambles reports in USD but is priced in AUD. A sustained appreciation of the AUD would reduce the AUD value of USD-denominated earnings and the IV per share.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Network effect + Switching costs + Scale/Cost advantages
**Moat strength:** Wide
**Moat trend:** Stable (narrowing risk: customer self-supply programs, but contained)

**FX note used throughout this report:** AUD/USD = 0.640; all USD figures converted at this rate where noted. USD 1 = AUD 1.5625.

**Network effect.** CHEP operates in approximately 60 countries with over 300 million pallets in circulation. The denser the network, the greater the collection efficiency and the faster pallets flow back to manufacturers. A competitor attempting to replicate this must simultaneously convince manufacturers and retailers to accept a new pallet standard — a chicken-and-egg problem that has prevented meaningful displacement for 40+ years. The network compounds: every new customer strengthens the value proposition for existing customers.

**Switching costs.** Brambles' customers (Nestlé, Unilever, P&G, Walmart, Woolworths) have integrated their supply chain operations — warehouse racking, conveyor systems, forklift specifications — around the CHEP standard 1,200 × 1,000mm pallet footprint. Switching to a competitor (LOSCAM, PECO, whitewood) involves retraining, equipment modifications, and supply disruptions. These costs are non-trivial and create significant inertia even when competitive alternatives exist.

**Scale/Cost advantages.** Brambles' existing collection network means its cost-per-pallet-trip is structurally lower than any competitor could achieve at smaller scale. Local density (number of CHEP service centres per geographic area) determines collection turnaround time, and CHEP's density in the Americas, Europe, and Asia-Pacific is unmatched.

**Five-year moat durability test.** In five years (by 2031), CHEP's network will be larger, its digital asset-tracking investment (RFID pallet tagging) will be further embedded, and the regulatory pressure on whitewood (non-hygiene-certified) pallets in food supply chains is likely to grow. Customer switching costs will not diminish. The principal threat — large CPG companies building proprietary pallet pools — is real but expensive; only a handful of companies (e.g., Costco in North America) have the scale to make this economical, and even those are partial. **Verdict: moat durable for 5+ years; wide designation maintained.**

### 13. Bridgewater Risk Assessment

**Interest rate sensitivity:** Moderate. Brambles carries USD 2.91bn of debt. A 1% rise in global interest rates would increase interest expense by ~USD 29m annually (if all floating), reducing net income by ~USD 20m after tax (AUD 31m), or ~AUD 0.023 per share — a 2% EPS impact. Debt is mixed fixed/floating; actual sensitivity may be lower. The stock's beta of 0.46 suggests the market has priced in lower macro sensitivity than average.

**Inflation sensitivity:** Mildly positive. Brambles' contracts include cost-pass-through clauses for wood (primary raw material) and labour. In practice, Brambles has demonstrated pricing power through inflationary periods (FY22–24), passing through cost increases with modest lag. EBITDA margins have been resilient. A deflationary pallet-price environment (falling wood costs without corresponding price reductions to customers) would be mildly accretive.

**Recession stress test:** Brambles' revenue is correlated with FMCG and grocery supply chain volumes — some of the most defensive consumer spending categories (people continue buying food in recessions). Historical drawdown in revenue during the GFC was modest (~3–5%). Estimated drawdown in a severe recession: **revenue −8% to −12%, earnings −15% to −25%** (operating leverage). Market drawdown in share price: estimated −20% to −35% from current levels (to AUD 12.70–15.70). This is the key risk scenario.

**Liquidity risk:** Low. Brambles has access to revolving credit facilities and maintains investment-grade credit ratings. Short-term maturities are manageable. Cash of USD 0.17bn is lean but liquidity is supplemented by undrawn facilities.

**Leverage risk:** Manageable. Net debt / EBITDA = 1.32× — within the self-imposed ~1.5× comfort zone. The US$400m buyback will modestly increase leverage, taking net debt/EBITDA toward ~1.5×; still manageable.
