## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside — with a clear explanation.** Brambles owns and operates CHEP, the world's largest pallet- and container-pooling business. The model is simple to explain to a twelve-year-old: instead of every manufacturer and retailer buying, storing, repairing and disposing of their own wooden pallets, Brambles owns a shared pool of millions of pallets that circulate between suppliers, distribution centres and retailers, and charges a rental/pooling fee for the service. It is infrastructure hiding inside a supply chain — closer to a toll road than a manufacturer. Revenue is a function of volume moved through customers' supply chains (largely FMCG, grocery, industrial), not commodity or technology risk. This sits squarely in the "infrastructure" bucket of the circle of competence list in this project's charter.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

1. **CHEP's pooling network retains its scale/switching-cost moat** even as the company insources pallet repair capacity away from third-party subcontractors (the driver of the May 2026 guidance cut).
2. **Underlying profit growth reaccelerates from the FY26-guided 3–5% (cc FX) back toward historical high-single-digit rates** once the repair-capacity transition is complete — this is the single biggest swing factor in the valuation (stage-1 growth assumption of 11.3% embeds a meaningful recovery).
3. **The USD-denominated earnings base translates into AUD without major structural FX shock** — assumed FX of ~1 AUD = 0.65 USD (≈1 USD = 1.54 AUD) held broadly steady over the forecast horizon.
4. **The new US$400m buy-back is executed roughly as announced**, providing EPS support independent of organic profit growth.
5. **No further downside surprises from the subcontractor transition** (no customer losses, no material litigation, no second guidance cut in H2 FY26).

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

- **Moat type:** Scale (largest global pallet pool, highest network density) + switching costs (CHEP pallet specifications and logistics are embedded in customers' supply-chain and warehouse-automation systems) + a network-effect flavour (pooling economics improve as network density rises, making it harder for a smaller rival to match cost-per-movement).
- **Strength: Wide.** This is corroborated independently by Morningstar's own Wide moat rating (see cross-check below) and by the financial evidence: ROE of 28.1% and net margin of 13.7% on an asset-intensive pooled-equipment base are not achievable without real pricing power and high barriers to replication (a rival would need to build a comparable multi-billion-dollar global pallet fleet from scratch and win density in every market simultaneously).
- **Trend: Stable, with a credible medium-term widening case.** The FY26 guidance cut stemmed from an operational transition — exiting third-party repair subcontractors in favour of company-controlled repair capacity — which is a moat-*reinforcing* move in principle (tighter quality control, better cost curve over time) but has near-term execution risk. The market's initial reaction (a 19.8% single-day drawdown on 17 May 2026) reflects genuine uncertainty about execution, not a moat impairment per se.
- **Five-year moat durability test:** Pass. The economics that make CHEP hard to replicate — density, embedded customer processes, capital intensity — are structural and don't depend on any single management decision. The repair-network transition is a tactical execution question, not a threat to the pooling model itself.

### 4. Management Assessment

- **Integrity:** No auto-veto triggers in the data provided — no fraud, restatement, or governance scandal, just an operational guidance miss.
- **Capital allocation track record:** The financial history shows real discipline over FY22–FY24: total debt fell from US$2,876m to US$2,641m while equity grew from US$2,451m to US$3,227m, taking D/E from ~1.17x to ~0.82x even as free cash flow swung from -US$86m (FY22, heavy capex on fleet/repair investment) to +US$655m (FY24) as capex normalised from US$1,672m to US$1,149m. That is a coherent multi-year deleveraging and cash-conversion story.
- **Owner mentality / capital return:** Layering a new US$400m buy-back on top of an existing program *at the same time* as cutting profit guidance is a mixed signal — either confidence that the cash generation is intact despite the earnings miss (FCF-before-dividends guidance of US$1,000–1,100m for FY26 supports this), or a play to cushion EPS optics through a lower share count. I read it as more the former than the latter given the deleveraging track record, but it is a **monitoring item**, not a clean pass.
- **Institutional imperative warning:** The magnitude of the guidance cut (underlying profit growth guidance roughly halved, from 8–11% to 3–5%) inside a single trading update is a forecasting-discipline red flag worth watching into H1 FY26 results — a repeat miss would be a genuine yellow card on management credibility, not just execution.

Terminal value blends perpetuity growth with a 15x exit multiple (quality tier: wide moat). Result: **DCF fair value AUD 21.77/share.**

**Method 2 — Owner-earnings multiple (quality tier).** Applying the wide-moat-tier 22x multiple to normalised owner earnings gives **AUD 18.91/share** — meaningfully below the DCF figure, reflecting that a static multiple doesn't capture the assumed medium-term margin recovery embedded in the DCF's growth path.

**Method 3 — Graham Number.** **Not meaningful here (n/a).** Graham's formula (√(22.5 × EPS × BVPS)) is built for asset-heavy, low-growth businesses where book value is a reasonable proxy for liquidation/replacement value. Brambles' book value per share (≈AUD 3.56, derived from price ÷ P/B) badly understates the earning power of the pooled-asset network — plugging trailing EPS of AUD 0.95 and BVPS of AUD 3.56 in gives ≈AUD 8.7/share, less than half the current price, which simply confirms the formula is the wrong tool for a high-ROE (28.1%) pooling/services business rather than telling us anything about mispricing. Correctly excluded from triangulation.

**Method 4 — Reverse DCF.** Solving for the stage-1 growth rate implied by the current AUD 20.33 price gives **9.6%**, versus the 11.3% assumed above. The market is pricing in a slightly more conservative recovery than the base case — a sensible, moderately skeptical stance given the guidance cut, and a useful sanity check that the 11.3% assumption is optimistic rather than absurd.

**Blended code fair value: AUD 20.34**, with a sensitivity-grid-derived IV range of **AUD 18.46–27.01** (cost of equity ±1%, terminal growth ±0.5%, unioned with method spread).

**My reconciled view.** My own IV midpoint of **AUD 21.25** (range AUD 18.50–24.00) sits only ~4.5% above the code model's AUD 20.34 and ~0.5% below the analyst consensus target of AUD 21.36 — all three converge tightly, and none of it triggers the ~20% divergence threshold that would require a full reconciliation write-up. Narrowing the top of the range reflects that the 27.01 tail requires both a rosier discount rate *and* a full return to the top of the growth range simultaneously — plausible, but not the central case a year after a guidance cut.
