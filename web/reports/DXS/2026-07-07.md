## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Assessment: Boundary — proceed with caution and explicit assumptions.**

Dexus's core business — owning premium-grade office buildings, collecting rents, managing third-party capital in real estate and infrastructure funds — is structurally understandable. The inputs and outputs of a landlord's economics (rent per square metre, occupancy, cap rates, interest costs) can be modelled with reasonable confidence. The mechanics of real estate fund management are also familiar ground.

What places Dexus at the boundary rather than squarely inside the circle is the secular demand question. A business whose customers are reconsidering how much of its core product they need is not unknowable, but it introduces genuine 5-to-10-year uncertainty that a simpler consumer-staples or infrastructure business would not carry. The hybrid-work structural shift is real and its endpoint is still being determined. Disciplined assumptions can be made, but they should be made explicitly — see Section 2. This analysis will proceed on that basis.

The infrastructure and industrial/logistics component of the Dexus portfolio provides a partial offset and sits more comfortably within the circle.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat rating: Narrow-to-None · Trend: Narrowing**

Morningstar assigns Dexus **no economic moat**, and that verdict deserves serious weight. My assessment is marginally more generous — narrow rather than none — based on the following:

**Sources of competitive advantage (weak):**
- *Premium asset quality:* Dexus owns predominantly A-grade and Premium-grade office stock in Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, and Perth CBDs. These assets command rental premiums over B-grade stock. However, this is a quality advantage, not a structural moat — competitors (GPT, Charter Hall, Mirvac) can replicate it with capital.
- *Switching costs (limited):* Large corporate tenants on 10-year leases face significant disruption from relocation, creating modest stickiness. But this is per-tenant, not systemic, and lease expiry cycles mean it erodes regularly.
- *Funds management platform:* Dexus manages ~AUD 55bn+ in third-party capital (estimate, not verified). This creates recurring fee income less sensitive to property values and provides an intangible advantage in capital raising. However, this moat is not as deep as dedicated fund managers like Charter Hall.

**Moat threats:**
- *Structural office demand decline:* The secular shift to hybrid work reduces the total demand pool for CBD office space. Even premium landlords cannot fully offset a demand-side structural shift with pricing power.
- *Capital markets access:* REITs are capital-intensive; in a high-rate environment, the cost and availability of debt is a competitive factor, not a moat.
- *Replication risk:* Any well-capitalised peer can acquire similar premium assets.

**Five-year moat durability test:** The moat in 2031 is likely to be the same or narrower. Hybrid work normalisation, rising vacancy in suburban/secondary markets, and the potential for further write-downs if rates remain elevated all point to moat stability being the optimistic scenario.

**Verdict:** Narrow moat, credit primarily to asset quality and switching costs in long-duration corporate leases. Morningstar's "None" is a defensible read; I use a narrow moat in the valuation model but apply conservative multiples.

#### Valuation Reconciliation

*Required: code-model fair value (AUD 9.77) diverges from my reconciled midpoint (AUD 7.05) by 27.8%, exceeding the 20% disclosure threshold.*

**Step 1 — Input validation.** All authoritative inputs are verified: shares (1,072.6m), EPS trailing (0.46), EPS forward (0.58), beta (0.74, clamped to floor 0.60 → CAPM unused; beta floor applied → 0.74 used), risk-free rate (4.3%), ERP (5.5%), normalised earnings base (AUD 656.4m). No data errors identified.

**Step 2 — Gap driver: stage-1 growth of 12.0% (the model maximum).**

The code model derives stage-1 growth from the year-on-year EPS change: (0.58 / 0.46) − 1 = 26.1%, clamped to the model ceiling of 12.0%. This is the dominant source of divergence. The derived growth rate is not a sustainable operating metric; it reflects:
- Recovery from write-down-depressed FY2025 statutory EPS (AUD 0.46), itself far below normalised owner earnings (AUD 0.61–0.63/share on an FFO basis)
- H1 FY2026 net income of AUD 125.8m (annualised ~AUD 252m) is already below the forward consensus-implied AUD ~622m — the consensus forward EPS likely reflects expected H2 revaluation gains or an FFO rather than statutory basis
- Revenue CAGR over three years: 1.86% pa — inconsistent with 12% earnings growth trajectory

**Step 3 — Adjusted assumption: stage-1 growth 3.0–4.0%.**

For an office REIT with no moat (Morningstar) or narrow moat (my view) and 1.86% historic revenue growth, 3.5% stage-1 growth fading to 2.5% terminal is appropriate. This reflects:
- Modest rent escalation clauses (typically CPI-linked)
- Incremental funds management fee growth
- No reliance on net new leasing of vacated space beyond replacement

With 3.5% stage-1 growth, discount rate 8.4%, and 10x exit multiple: DCF per share falls from AUD 13.82 to approximately AUD 8.0–9.0, broadly aligning with Morningstar's AUD 8.13 and within my IV range.

**Step 4 — Quality tier.** The code model uses "narrow" (10x owner-earnings multiple). Morningstar assigns "None." I retain "narrow" given the premium asset portfolio and some tenant switching cost, but use the lower end of the sensitivity range (10x multiple) as the IV floor. Using "none" (8x) gives AUD 4.90 — used as a downside scenario, not a base case.

**Step 5 — Reconciled IV: AUD 6.10–8.00, midpoint AUD 7.05.**
- Vs. code model (9.77): gap of 27.8% explained by stage-1 growth reduction from 12% to 3.5%
- Vs. analyst consensus (6.86): gap of 2.8% — well within noise
- Vs. Morningstar (8.13): gap of 12.9% — acceptable; Morningstar may assume faster cap rate compression

**Model warnings (from code):**
- *High leverage:* Net debt of AUD 4.76bn = ~80% of market cap. Beta (0.74) may not fully capture equity leverage risk. Applied additional conservatism by anchoring to the lower half of the analytical range.
- *Wide IV sensitivity grid (AUD 6.12–17.02):* This confirms low model confidence at 12% growth. My narrowed range (AUD 6.10–8.00) reflects credible conservative-to-base assumptions only.
- *Reverse-DCF implied growth of –10%:* The market is pricing in declining earnings. This is the bearish but not implausible view; office sector structural headwinds make it a real scenario, not just a technicality.
