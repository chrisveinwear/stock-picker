## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside, with caveats.** EBOS Group is a pharmaceutical, medical, and healthcare-products wholesale distributor plus a smaller animal-care/consumer-health manufacturing arm — the core model (buy in bulk from manufacturers, warehouse, and deliver to thousands of pharmacies and hospitals faster and cheaper than any single customer could do alone) is a simple, explainable business: it is essentially plumbing for the healthcare supply chain. That is squarely within the "healthcare — hospitals, pathology, pharmaceuticals" and "consumer staples-like distribution" territory this framework is comfortable with. The caveat: EBOS's economics are set largely by contract-renewal cycles with a handful of very large pharmacy customers, and the loss or renegotiation of any single major distribution relationship can swing group revenue by high single digits overnight, as the FY25 numbers below illustrate. That concentration risk is understandable, but it makes the business more binary than a typical staples distributor — worth flagging up front rather than after the valuation work.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

1. EBOS retains its position as one of the two dominant full-line pharmaceutical wholesalers in Australia/NZ and does not lose further major distribution contracts beyond what is already reflected in FY25 revenue.
2. Group revenue stabilises around the FY25 base (AUD 13.2bn) and resumes low-to-mid single-digit growth from FY26 onward, rather than continuing the FY24→FY25 decline.
3. Net margins hold in the 1.8–2.1% band seen over FY22–FY25 (distribution economics are thin-margin/high-turnover by nature; there is no evidence of imminent margin expansion).
4. Capex intensity continues to normalise lower (capex fell from AUD 107m in FY23 to AUD 54m in FY25), supporting the recent FCF recovery to AUD 396m.
5. Total debt stays broadly flat at ~AUD 1.7–1.9bn and D/E near 0.68x, i.e. no material re-leveraging for M&A.

If assumption 1 or 2 breaks — another large contract loss, or a further step-down in revenue — the valuation case weakens materially, since owner earnings are already being carried by cost discipline rather than growth.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Scale/cost + switching costs (logistics network density, integrated ordering systems embedded in customer pharmacies, regulatory/wholesale-licensing barriers to entry). Not an intangible-asset or network-effect moat.
**Strength:** Narrow — this matches the independent Morningstar assessment (Narrow moat) imported for this stock, and the financial evidence supports it: ROE of ~9.0% and net margins under 2% are well below what a wide-moat business would generate. A distributor earns its return on turnover and working-capital efficiency, not pricing power.
**Trend:** Narrowing to stable. The FY24→FY25 revenue decline (AUD 14,411m → 13,209m, −8.3%) alongside a 21.9% fall in net income (AUD 297m → 232m) is the kind of customer-concentration event that narrow-moat distributors are structurally exposed to — a large counterparty can walk. FCF actually *improved* over the same period (AUD 288m → 396m) purely on capex discipline, which is a management-quality positive but does not offset the top-line signal.
**Five-year moat durability test:** Would EBOS still be the logistics backbone for Australian/NZ pharmacy in 2031? Probably yes for the *majority* of the network — switching an entire wholesale relationship is operationally painful for a pharmacy group — but the FY25 experience shows the moat is not airtight against a large, motivated counterparty (e.g., one vertically integrating its own distribution). Durable but not impregnable.

Total debt has been kept in a fairly narrow band (AUD 1.4bn–1.8bn) rather than spiking to defend market share, which is a sign of financial conservatism.
**Owner mentality:** Not directly measurable from the supplied data (insider ownership not available — see section 16); the balance-sheet conservatism is a reasonable proxy for a "protect the downside" mindset.
**Institutional imperative warnings:** None evident from the data — no sign of empire-building acquisitions inflating the balance sheet; debt has actually fallen from its FY22 peak.

### 6. Valuation & Margin of Safety

**Method 1 — DCF (owner earnings):** The canonical model (dcf-v2) discounts normalised owner earnings (median of forward-NI, margin-normalised, historical owner-earnings, and historical-FCF candidates → AUD 280.4m base) at an 8.0% CAPM cost of equity (beta clamped from an unrealistically low 0.15 up to the 0.60 floor; raw cost of equity of 7.6% floored to the 8.0% policy minimum). With 12% stage-1 growth fading to a 2.5% terminal rate over 10 years, the standalone DCF value is **AUD 34.04** — the model itself flags this as low-confidence (IV swings 40% across the sensitivity grid).

**Method 2 — Owner-earnings multiple:** Applying the quality-tier ("average" tier, 12.5x) multiple to normalised owner earnings gives **AUD 17.10**.

**Method 3 — Graham Number:** √(22.5 × trailing EPS × book value/share) = √(22.5 × 1.02 × 14.39) = **AUD 18.17** (book value/share = AUD 2,950m equity ÷ 205.0m shares = AUD 14.39). This is a useful independent check and happens to equal the code's blended fair value almost exactly.

**Method 4 — Reverse DCF:** Solving for the stage-1 growth rate implied by the current AUD 18.54 price returns **−2.6%** — the market is pricing EBOS as though owner earnings *shrink* for the next several years, not grow at the 12% the forward-analyst-derived assumption implies.

**Valuation Reconciliation (assumption-level, not structural):** The four methods span AUD 17.10–34.04, a huge range for the same business. The disciplined read here is that the raw DCF's 12% stage-1 growth assumption is the outlier that needs challenging, not the model shape. That 12% figure comes from a forward-vs-trailing-EPS derivation, but it sits awkwardly against the actual revenue trend in the financial history: FY22–FY25 revenue CAGR is only ~3.5%, and FY25 revenue *fell* 8.3% year-on-year on the loss of scale in the distribution book. A business that just lost 8% of its top line is not a 12%-owner-earnings-grower over the next decade absent evidence of a specific recovery catalyst (none is supplied here). The reverse-DCF result reinforces this: the market's implied growth expectation is negative, not +12%. Downweighting the raw DCF and leaning on the Graham number (AUD 18.17) and owner-earnings multiple (AUD 17.10) — both of which are far closer to each other and to the current price — is the more defensible read, consistent with the code model's own blended fair value of AUD 18.17 (a divergence of only ~13% from my reconciled midpoint, well inside the reconciliation threshold). I widen modestly above that floor to account for the genuine, capex-driven FCF improvement and the possibility that FY25's revenue step-down proves a one-off rather than a trend.

**Reconciled intrinsic value range: AUD 17–24, midpoint AUD 20.50.** At AUD 18.54, margin of safety versus the midpoint is **9.6%** — versus the code model's IV range (AUD 17.10–42.44, fair value AUD 18.17) and versus the analyst consensus target of AUD 24.48. This is consistent with, not a large divergence from, either external anchor (13% from code fair value, 16% below consensus), so no forced structural adjustment is required.

**Recommended entry price:** at or below AUD 16.50 (implies ≥30% discount to the range low), matching consensusBuyBelow.
