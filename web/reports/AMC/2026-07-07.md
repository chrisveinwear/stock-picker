## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

---

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Assessment: Inside the circle, with one important caveat.**

Amcor's core business is straightforward to explain: it makes the flexible and rigid plastic, aluminium, and fibre packaging in which consumer staples companies ship their products. It sells Heinz the pouch for the ketchup, the blister pack for the tablet, the wrapper for the protein bar. End customers do not choose the packaging — the brand owner does — and once a customer has co-designed packaging for a product line, switching is expensive in time, tooling, and regulatory requalification. This is a manufacturing and logistics business dressed up as a materials business, and the economics are understandable.

The caveat: the addition of Berry Global's predominantly rigid-plastics business means Amcor now spans product categories (closures, containers, caps, protective industrial packaging) that are further from the core flexible-film franchise, and the integration is still live. Investors must hold the caveat in mind without losing sight of the underlying simplicity: Amcor makes containers for things people consume daily.

**Conclusion:** Inside the circle. Proceed.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5 Thesis Drivers)

The investment thesis rests on five assumptions that must be monitored and verified each half-year:

1. **Berry synergies are realised on schedule.** Amcor guided USD 530m+ of annualised synergies by FY2027, to be achieved through procurement rationalisation, manufacturing footprint consolidation, and SG&A reduction. This is the single largest swing factor for earnings normalisation. If synergies land at two-thirds of guidance (USD 350m), forward EPS falls materially.

2. **FCF recovers to USD 1.2–1.5bn by FY2027.** The TTM FCF of USD 0.15bn and FY2025 FCF of USD 0.81bn reflect elevated integration capex and working capital absorption. Normalised FCF at USD 1.2–1.5bn is required for the dividend (~USD 1.1bn annual payout) to be self-funding.

3. **Net debt/EBITDA trends toward 3.5× within three years.** At ~4.7× today, Amcor must generate growing EBITDA and direct free cash flow toward debt reduction. Leverage at current levels is incompatible with the 30% MOS safety standard for a Buffett-quality holding.

4. **End-market volumes stabilise and partially recover.** The FY2024 revenue dip (to USD 13.6bn from USD 14.7bn) reflected consumer destocking in Amcor's legacy markets. H1 FY2026 showed 68% revenue growth (Berry-driven) with management reaffirming guidance, suggesting volumes are stable.

5. **AUD/USD remains in the 0.60–0.68 range.** Amcor reports in USD; the ASX price is AUD. A materially stronger AUD compresses the AUD price of intrinsic value (which scales with USD earnings) without any business change.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Switching costs (primary) + Cost/scale (secondary)  
**Moat strength:** Narrow (confirmed by Morningstar's narrow-moat rating as at 28 June 2026)  
**Moat trend:** Stable, with short-term execution risk from Berry integration

**Switching costs:** Flexible packaging is co-engineered with the customer's product. Amcor typically operates co-located "pouch factories" adjacent to major customer manufacturing sites — walking away means re-qualifying packaging with regulatory bodies (especially in pharmaceutical and food applications), re-tooling machinery, and re-certifying supply chains. These costs are low in absolute dollar terms but high relative to the incremental savings a brand owner would achieve from switching. This is the moat's engine: stickiness, not pricing power.

**Scale/cost advantages:** At USD 22bn in revenue, the combined Amcor-Berry entity is the world's largest consumer packaging company. Procurement scale for resins, aluminium, and energy creates 2–4% cost advantages versus mid-tier competitors. Global manufacturing footprint (260+ plants in 40+ countries) means Amcor can service multinational food and beverage companies across all their production markets — a capability no smaller competitor can replicate.

**Moat limitations:** Packaging is ultimately commoditised at the margin. Private-label film suppliers compete on price in less specialised applications. The gross margin of 19.1% and operating margin of 8.6% are narrow, consistent with a business that earns returns modestly above its cost of capital rather than far above it. The moat is real but thin.

**Five-year moat durability test:** Customer contracts are typically 3–5 years. The shift toward sustainable packaging (recyclable, compostable materials) creates some re-evaluation risk — if Amcor is slow to lead the transition, customers have a legitimate reason to re-tender. Management has invested in sustainability credentials and holds leading market share in recyclable flexible packaging, mitigating this risk. The moat should be stable over five years barring a regulatory shock to single-use plastics.

**Pre-Berry ROE (3yr average FY2022–FY2024):** ~21.5% — consistent with a business earning well above its cost of equity on the legacy portfolio. Post-Berry ROE has collapsed to 8.7% due to the diluted equity base; the path back toward 15%+ is the core value-unlock thesis.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity:** No material red flags identified. Amcor has a consistent track record of transparent financial reporting across multiple jurisdictions (NYSE, ASX, SEC filings). Management guided FY2026 earnings at H1 results and confirmed guidance on H1 FY2026 revenue results, which came in at +68% as flagged. No accounting irregularities or related-party concerns noted.

**Capital allocation track record:** Mixed. Pre-Berry Amcor was a disciplined capital allocator — steady dividend growth, consistent maintenance capex, bolt-on acquisitions of packaging businesses that added capability without balance-sheet stress. The Berry Global acquisition is categorically different: a USD ~15bn deal financed primarily with assumed debt that has taken the net debt/EBITDA ratio from ~3.5× to ~4.7×. The deal's strategic logic is sound (scale is structurally important in packaging); the execution risk is significant.

**Owner mentality:** The management team (CEO Ron Delia, who has led the company since 2015) has substantial skin in the game and has consistently framed capital allocation decisions in per-share terms. The maintenance of the dividend at current levels (~USD 1.1bn/year) when FCF temporarily fell below the payout is a signal that management believes in the dividend's sustainability — a commitment worth watching, not applauding until FCF coverage is restored.

**Institutional imperative warning:** The Berry acquisition has the hallmarks of empire-building. At ~2× revenue of the legacy business, it stretched Amcor beyond its core flexible-film franchise into rigid containers and industrial packaging. The synergy target of USD 530m is achievable but requires flawless execution of a complex cross-cultural integration. Charlie Munger's warning about "big bold moves" resonates here. Management must be held to quarterly milestones.

**Verdict on management:** Competent and trustworthy, but the Berry deal tests the boundary of acceptable capital allocation for a value investor. No automatic veto — but the leverage situation means management is running at reduced margin of error.

### 13. Bridgewater Risk Assessment

**Inflation sensitivity: MODERATE (manageable with lag)**  
Amcor's primary raw material inputs are polymer resins, aluminium, and energy. During FY2023–FY2024, sharp input cost inflation was partially passed through to customers but with a 3–6 month lag (contract price resets). The gross margin dipped to 19.1% (TTM) from higher levels. In a re-inflationary environment, Amcor would absorb a margin hit for 1–2 quarters before repricing; the narrow gross margin means these hits are felt more acutely than in higher-margin businesses.

**Recession stress test:**  
Consumer staples packaging is among the most defensive demand categories — people still need food packaging in a recession. - Revenue −20%: EBITDA barely covers interest — equity value severely impaired
- **Estimated drawdown in a genuine recession:** 30–40% from current levels

The ~USD 3.2bn EBITDA provides debt service capacity, but execution must be flawless.

**Leverage risk: HIGH**  
Net debt/EBITDA ~4.7× (estimated: USD 15.17bn net debt / USD 3.20bn EBITDA). This exceeds the typical packaging sector comfort zone of 2.5–3.5×. Management's target of <3.5× is achievable by FY2028 if FCF builds as forecast, but it requires three years of disciplined capital allocation — no further M&A, dividend maintained at current levels rather than grown, and synergies delivered.

Australian CPI is tracking toward the RBA's 2–3% target band in 2026 after the 2022–2024 inflation cycle. GDP growth is estimated at 2–2.5%. This is a stable macro backdrop for consumer staples packaging — demand for food and beverage packaging remains driven by population growth (~1.5%/year) and per-capita consumption patterns rather than cyclical GDP swings.

**AUD strength and currency impact:**  
Amcor reports in USD. The ASX price of AMC.AX is the AUD equivalent of the USD earnings stream. A strengthening AUD (e.g., AUD/USD rising from 0.635 to 0.70) reduces the AUD-equivalent intrinsic value by approximately 9.5% — not because the business changed, but because each USD of earnings translates to fewer AUD. This is the most direct macro risk for Australian investors in Amcor. Conversely, RBA rate cuts tend to weaken the AUD, supporting the AUD-denominated price.

**Sector rotation signals:**  
The packaging sector sits at the intersection of Consumer Staples (defensive) and Industrials (cyclical). In the current environment — falling rates, moderating inflation, stable consumer spending — packaging stocks historically benefit from: (a) income rotation (yield above bonds attracts income funds) and (b) margin recovery as input cost pressures ease. The FY2024 destocking cycle that compressed Amcor's volumes appears to have run its course; a reflation of packaging volumes is consistent with a global consumer recovery narrative.

**Macro tailwinds for Amcor (specific):**

3. Sustainability transition: the shift from unrecyclable to recyclable and compostable packaging materials provides Amcor with a product upgrade cycle — existing customers replacing current packaging with higher-value sustainable alternatives.

**Macro headwinds for Amcor (specific):**
1. Plastic regulation risk: growing regulatory pressure on single-use plastics in Europe, Australia, and elsewhere. Amcor has invested in recyclable solutions, but a faster-than-expected regulatory tightening could strand capital in legacy assets.
2. Deglobalisation: tariff walls and supply-chain regionalisation fragment Amcor's global customer base, increasing the complexity and cost of serving multinationals across multiple production geographies.
3. USD/AUD translation: as noted, AUD appreciation directly reduces the AUD intrinsic value of this USD-reporting business.

- Global consumer volume recovery: already beginning (H1 FY2026 guidance reaffirmed); full effect in FY2027 results
