# Amcor plc (AMC.AX) — Investment Research Report

**VERDICT: Watch** — This is a competent global packaging franchise at a price offering no margin of safety; the 6.2% dividend is real, the earnings recovery is probable, but a balance sheet carrying USD 15 billion in debt leaves no room for error if the integration stumbles.
**Price:** AUD 61.67 | **IV Range:** AUD 48–70 | **Margin of Safety:** −4.5%

## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside the circle.**

Amcor manufactures flexible and rigid packaging for consumer staples, healthcare, and beverage customers worldwide. The business model is transparent: source polymer resins and aluminium foil as commodity inputs, convert them into packaging film, pouches, bottles, and tubes on long-term supply agreements with blue-chip FMCG and pharmaceutical companies, and earn a manufacturing margin from precision, scale, and customer intimacy. Revenue tracks consumer consumption volumes — not economic cycles in any material way. The value chain is clear: buy commodity, add manufacturing precision and global scale, sell under multi-year contractual relationships with Nestlé, Heinz, Unilever, and Johnson & Johnson.

Amcor is incorporated in Jersey, headquartered in Zurich, dual-listed on the ASX (AMC) and NYSE (AMCR), and reports in USD. None of that complexity changes what the business does: it keeps products fresh and safe. There are no hidden derivatives books, no regulatory cross-subsidies, and no revenue recognition complexity. Packaging is no more conceptually difficult than See's Candies selling boxes of chocolate — and like See's, the economics are driven by customer retention, not invention.

### 2. Key Assumptions (5)

1. **Earnings recover to AUD ~4.75/share normalised** within 2–3 years, representing approximately 76% of the AUD 6.24 forward EPS consensus — integration costs and working capital drag unwind toward steady state as the Bemis integration matures.
2. **Net leverage de-rates from ~4.7× to ~3.5× EBITDA** within 36 months through FCF generation and disciplined capex; no large acquisitions while leverage remains elevated.
3. **Consumer staples and healthcare packaging volumes remain resilient** — flat to +2% organic growth in core markets; no structural substitution toward alternative materials at the scale of Amcor's largest contracts.
4. **The dividend is maintained at ~6.2% yield** (approximately AUD 3.82/share); forward payout ratio of ~61% on AUD 6.24 forward EPS is manageable provided earnings recovery proceeds as projected.
5. **No material near-term regulatory shock** on single-use plastics across key markets (EU, US, ANZ) before Amcor's sustainable packaging transition — recyclable and fibre-based alternatives — is commercially viable at scale.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

- **Moat type:** Customer switching costs + scale cost advantage + global manufacturing network
- **Strength:** Narrow | **Trend:** Stable

Amcor clears the narrow-moat bar comfortably but does not approach wide-moat territory. Three genuine sources of competitive advantage exist. First, **customer inertia**: FMCG customers invest in dedicated tooling, regulatory filings, and supply chain integration for specific packaging formats; re-qualifying a new supplier requires 12–24 months and risks production disruption. This is a real barrier but not an impregnable one — a sufficiently motivated customer can switch. Second, **procurement and manufacturing scale**: as the world's largest flexible packaging producer, Amcor purchases resins at prices smaller competitors cannot match and amortises fixed manufacturing costs across 220+ facilities in 40+ countries. Third, **geographic reach**: local factories in 40+ countries insulate customers from supply chain disruption; replicating this footprint would require decades and tens of billions.

What prevents a wide-moat rating: gross margin of 19.1% and operating margin of 8.6% are thin by franchise standards. A true franchise business can raise prices freely; Amcor can raise prices formulaically within contract structures, which is a different and weaker power. ROE of 8.7% is well below Buffett's 15% hurdle and is inconsistent with the excess returns a wide moat should generate. The business is partly commodity-adjacent — resin cost movements flow through the income statement, and Amcor's pricing power is contractual-formulaic rather than brand-based.

**Five-year moat durability test:** Stable to modest improvement. Tightening sustainability regulations (EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, equivalent local legislation) are closing the door on smaller players that lack R&D budgets for recyclable structures. Amcor's scale and existing investment in sustainable packaging is a consolidating force. The moat is not widening rapidly, but it is not narrowing; in a regulated transition, large players often emerge stronger.

Applying Buffett's "hypothetical competitor" test: a well-capitalised new entrant could build a competitive flexible packaging operation in key geographies given 10+ years and tens of billions — but the customer relationships, regulatory approvals, and tooling incumbency mean they would compete at the margin, not displace Amcor. That is the definition of a narrow, durable moat.

### 4. Management Assessment

- **Integrity:** Pass — no governance red flags identified
- **Capital allocation:** Adequate, with reservations on leverage tolerance
- **Owner mentality:** Moderate
- **Institutional imperative:** Present and material

CEO Ron Delia (in post since 2015) has built Amcor from a ~USD 9bn-revenue regional player into a ~USD 22bn global leader primarily through the transformative 2019 Bemis Company acquisition (~USD 6.8bn) and subsequent bolt-ons. The Bemis integration delivered promised cost synergies and expanded the Americas flexible footprint considerably. The capital allocation track record is therefore mixed: the strategic logic was sound, the execution credible, but the resulting leverage (net debt/EBITDA now ~4.7×) has constrained optionality in a higher-rate environment.

The institutional imperative is visible in the acquisition history — management pursued deal-making while leverage remained above its stated target range. The glass-half-full interpretation is that the strategy was deliberately sequenced: acquire scale, integrate, then deleverage via FCF. The glass-half-empty interpretation is that the balance sheet is now Amcor's primary constraint, and any macro shock arrives with limited financial cushion. Management has publicly guided to a 3.0–3.5× net debt/EBITDA target; credibility rides entirely on delivering this over the next 12–24 months.

The strongest positive management signal is the dividend record: Amcor has maintained and grown its dividend for 25+ consecutive years — through the GFC, COVID, and the Bemis integration. This is a substantive capital allocation commitment backed by cash outflows, not a talking point. No accounting red flags, no material governance concerns, no related-party irregularities have been identified. The integrity test passes.

### 13. Bridgewater Risk Assessment

**Interest rate sensitivity: Negative — high severity.**
Net debt ~USD 15.17bn at an estimated blended borrowing rate of ~5.5% generates ~USD 0.92bn annual interest. Each +100bp increase in all-in borrowing costs adds ~USD 167m to interest expense, directly compressing an already-thin interest coverage ratio of 2.1×. Amcor's primary rate exposure is to the US Federal Reserve, not the RBA — debt is predominantly USD and EUR denominated. Fed rate cuts are a meaningful tailwind for refinancing; any reversion to higher-for-longer materially increases financial risk.

**Inflation sensitivity: Mixed.**
Amcor's contracts typically include raw material pass-through mechanisms with 3–6 month contractual lags. In high-inflation environments there is short-term margin compression, but no permanent structural damage — the cost base resets when contracts reprice. The greater inflation risk is second-order: sustained cost-of-living pressure on Amcor's FMCG customers could reduce consumer volumes on packaged goods, modestly reducing Amcor's volumes with it.

**Recession stress test:**
- Revenue −30%: Falls to USD ~15.5bn
- EBITDA (at maintained ~14% margin): ~USD 2.17bn
- Net leverage: 15.17 / 2.17 = **6.99× — likely covenant breach territory**
- Estimated equity drawdown: **−30 to −40%** (gearing amplifies equity losses non-linearly)
- Recovery horizon: 18–30 months assuming a V-shaped recovery

**Liquidity risk: Moderate-High.** FCF of USD 0.15bn provides minimal buffer. Revolving credit facilities provide short-term liquidity cushion. No near-term maturity wall has been publicly flagged, but covenant risk in a downturn is real and the key risk to any highly leveraged equity.

### 17. McKinsey Macro Context (Australia)

**RBA rate outlook:**
The RBA began cutting rates in late 2025 and further reductions are anticipated through 2026 as Australian CPI moderates toward the 2–3% target band. Rate cuts benefit Amcor through three channels: (1) sentiment lift for high-yield, high-leverage equities as income investors reallocate from cash to yield-generating equities; (2) reduced hedging costs on USD-denominated debt when AUD borrowing costs fall; (3) improved consumer confidence supporting FMCG volumes. The primary rate exposure, however, is to the US Federal Reserve (USD and EUR debt) — the RBA is a secondary rather than primary driver.

**Australian GDP and inflation:**
- Australian GDP growth: ~2.5% in FY26 — moderate recovery from subdued 2024–25 period; neutral-to-positive for packaging volumes
- Australian CPI: ~3.0% and declining — reducing consumer cost-of-living pressure; positive for branded goods volumes
- Household spending: Recovering cautiously; no V-shaped rebound expected, but the downside pressure from rate hikes is fading

**AUD strength impact:**
AUD/USD at approximately 0.64 — moderately weak by historical standards. AUD appreciation is a material hidden risk: Amcor earns USD and reports USD financials, but its ASX-listed shares are priced in AUD. If AUD strengthens toward 0.70 (plausible in a risk-on 12–18 month scenario), forward USD EPS of USD 3.99 converts to AUD 5.70 at 0.70 rather than AUD 6.24 at 0.64 — an 8.6% mechanical reduction in AUD EPS with no change in the underlying business. This FX risk is often underappreciated by AUD-focused income investors in Amcor.

**Sector rotation signals:**
Value and income rotations — which favour high-yield, low-beta defensive industrials — are supportive in the current environment. Falling Australian 10-year bond yields (currently ~4.3%) increase the attractiveness of Amcor's 6.2% yield and support P/E multiple expansion. Any reversion to rising yields would be a headwind.

**Macro tailwinds (with timeline):**
1. **Healthcare packaging growth** (ongoing, structural): Aging populations across the US, EU, and Japan drive pharmaceutical and medical packaging volume; Amcor is deliberately growing this segment; 3–5 year secular tailwind
2. **Sustainability regulation** (1–3 years): EU PPWR and equivalent national legislation impose compliance costs that Amcor's scale absorbs better than smaller competitors, accelerating market consolidation
3. **Manufacturing reshoring** (2–4 years): FMCG companies seeking regional supply chain resilience increases value of Amcor's local-factory footprint
4. **RBA rate cuts** (immediate to 12 months): Sentiment and yield-spread benefit, as noted

**Macro headwinds (with timeline):**
1. **AUD appreciation** (immediate): Each cent of AUD strengthening mechanically reduces AUD EPS; risk is concentrated in FX, not operations
2. **Resin/polymer cost inflation** (0–12 months): Oil price shocks or supply disruptions create 3–6 month margin pressure windows before contract repricing
3. **FMCG volume weakness** (0–18 months): If consumer spending disappoints globally, Amcor's volumes track down; defensive but not immune
4. **Plastics substitution** (3–7 years): Fibre-based and biodegradable packaging alternatives are advancing; the pace of substitution at Amcor's contract scale remains manageable for now

**McKinsey assessment:** The Australian macro backdrop is broadly supportive — falling rates, moderate growth, recovering consumer sentiment. The most important macro variable is USD/AUD — an appreciating Australian dollar is the primary near-term headwind that investors should monitor. The structural tailwinds from healthcare packaging and sustainability regulation are real but multi-year in nature.
