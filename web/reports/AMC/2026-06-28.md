## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside.** Consumer and industrial packaging is an entirely legible business. Amcor buys polymer, aluminium, and paper inputs, converts them into flexible film and rigid containers in high-volume factories, and sells them to FMCG companies under multi-year supply agreements that are renewed as a matter of course. The pricing mechanism is transparent — largely cost-plus with a conversion margin — and the competitive dynamics are straightforward: scale, reliability, and proximity to customers' factories.The Berry acquisition adds rigid packaging (bottles, closures, containers) to the legacy flexible packaging franchise — different manufacturing processes, same fundamental model. Both sit inside the circle.

### 2. Key Assumptions (thesis depends on these)

1. **Berry synergies are achievable.** Management targets USD 650m in annual run-rate synergies by FY28. Our IV midpoint assumes ~70% realization (USD 455m). If only 50% is realized, fair value falls approximately AUD 8 per share.
2. **FCF normalizes to USD 1.3–1.7bn by FY28.** Current FCF of USD 0.15bn is distorted by integration capex and working capital absorption. The USD 1.12bn annual dividend requires FCF at least doubling by FY27 to be self-funding.
3. **Debt reduction trajectory holds.** Net debt/EBITDA of 4.7× must fall to ≤3.0× by FY29. This requires EBITDA growth plus disciplined capital allocation — any slip delays deleveraging and keeps interest coverage at dangerously thin levels.
4. **Plastics transition does not accelerate materially.** ESG-driven customer migration from plastic to paper/fibre is a structural headwind. Our model assumes gradual transition over a decade, not step-change regulatory action in the near term.
5. **Organic volume growth 2–3% p.a.** Consumer staples packaging volumes are structurally stable; modest pricing and product-mix improvement sustains low-single-digit organic revenue growth independent of Berry synergies.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Scale economies + customer switching costs
**Moat strength:** Narrow
**Moat trend:** Stable to narrowing (sustainability transition introduces long-run uncertainty)

Post-Berry, Amcor operates 400+ facilities across 40+ countries and is the world's largest packaging company by revenue. The scale advantages are real: global polymer procurement, R&D cost amortized over USD 22bn in revenue, and the ability to offer FMCG multinationals a single global supply partner. Losing an Amcor account means the customer must re-qualify hundreds of SKUs across new suppliers — a costly, multi-year process that creates meaningful stickiness. P&G, Nestlé, Unilever, and AB InBev are anchor customers.

The moat is narrow rather than wide for three reasons. First, packaging inputs are commodities — Amcor's pricing power is limited to the conversion and service premium. Second, regional competitors (Sealed Air, Huhtamaki, Berry legacy facilities now absorbed) can replicate individual facilities at sufficient scale. Third, the sustainability transition introduces genuine long-run asset risk: if flexible plastic packaging loses favour, a meaningful portion of Amcor's invested capital faces accelerating obsolescence.

**Five-year moat durability test:** The scale moat is stable through 2031. The switching-cost moat is intact as long as FMCG customers remain on multi-year supply agreements. The sustainability transition is unlikely to inflect sharply within five years. Narrow moat rating stands.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity:** No red flags. The company has maintained transparent communication through the Berry transaction — a deal of this complexity creates ample opportunity for obfuscation, and management has not taken that path. Guidance was reaffirmed after H1 FY26 results despite material integration noise; that is a positive signal.

**Capital allocation:** The Berry acquisition is the defining capital allocation decision. Buying Berry at roughly 8× EV/EBITDA pre-synergies is not cheap, and taking leverage from ~2.5× to ~4.7× EBITDA in one transaction is aggressive. We flag the institutional imperative risk: management may have felt pressure to execute a transformative deal to justify its global ambitions and compensation structure. The deal's strategic logic (scale, procurement, customer breadth) is sound; the execution risk is real.

**Owner mentality:** Management has positioned the dividend as a core shareholder covenant, not merely a policy. The commitment to maintain distributions through a highly leveraged integration period signals owner-minded thinking — but also concentrates risk: if FCF disappoints, the choice between cutting the dividend and slowing debt repayment will be uncomfortable.

**Verdict:** Adequate — no integrity veto, but the debt load requires near-flawless execution through FY28.
