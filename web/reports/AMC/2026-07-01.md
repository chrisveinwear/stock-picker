## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

---

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Verdict: Inside the circle**

Amcor manufactures flexible and rigid packaging — the wrappers, pouches, bottles, caps, and closures that carry the world's food, beverage, pharmaceutical, and personal-care products from factory to shelf. The business model is straightforward: transform commodity raw materials (polyethylene, polypropylene, aluminium foil, PET resin) into precision-engineered packaging through continuous manufacturing lines, sell to blue-chip multinational consumer goods companies under multi-year supply contracts, and earn a conversion margin on top of pass-through raw-material costs.

The economics can be explained simply: Amcor provides a low-cost component (packaging represents 1–5% of finished goods value) that is operationally critical to its customers. Switching costs are meaningful — qualifications, tooling, validation cycles, and supply-chain disruption risk keep customers sticky. Volume growth broadly tracks global packaged goods consumption.

The Berry Global acquisition (closed mid-2025) added significant rigid packaging capability, particularly in North America, but does not change the fundamental simplicity of the model. It does complicate the balance sheet and the near-term cash-flow picture, which I must account for.

I can assess this business. No need to stop here.

---

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5 thesis-critical items)

These are the assumptions the bull case depends on; verify each quarter.

1. **Berry integration delivers USD 650m+ in annualised synergies by FY2028.** Management has guided to this figure. If synergies fall short by 25% or more, the acquisition rationale collapses and the debt load becomes very difficult.

2. **GAAP FCF normalises to USD 900m–1.2bn by FY2028.** Current GAAP FCF of USD 0.15bn is heavily distorted by integration costs, restructuring charges, and working-capital movements from the Berry deal. The business model supports higher cash generation; the assumption is that one-time costs clear by FY2027–28.

3. **Net debt/EBITDA deleverages from ~4.7x today to 3.0x or below by FY2029** through a combination of EBITDA growth (synergies + organic) and FCF directed to debt reduction. If leverage stays above 4x, the dividend and credit rating are both at risk.

4. **Adjusted EPS reaches AUD 7.00+ by FY2028**, implying the forward consensus of AUD 6.25 for FY2026 is a conservative baseline, not a ceiling. This requires organic volume growth of 2–3% annually and operating leverage on the enlarged cost base.

5. **The 6.1% dividend is maintained and grows modestly.** The GAAP payout is not covered by current GAAP FCF (USD 0.15bn FCF vs ~USD 1.15bn total dividend). Management is paying from adjusted/underlying FCF, which is substantially higher. The assumption is that adjusted FCF growth makes the dividend sustainable and progressively safer.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat rating: Narrow (confirmed by Morningstar) | Trend: Stable, with modest erosion risk from commoditisation**

Amcor possesses a narrow economic moat built on four interlocking sources:

**Customer switching costs.** Packaging is physically integrated into customer production lines. Changing a pouch supplier requires new tooling, shelf-life and migration testing, regulatory re-registration (especially in pharmaceutical packaging), and months of qualification time. For large consumer goods companies producing millions of units daily, even a modest supply disruption risk acts as a powerful retention mechanism. Once Amcor is embedded in a Nestlé or P&G production line, inertia is substantial.

**Scale and global manufacturing network.** With ~USD 22bn in revenue and operations across 40+ countries (post-Berry), Amcor can serve multinationals across geographies from a single supplier relationship, simplify their procurement, and meet local regulatory requirements simultaneously. This global breadth is difficult and capital-intensive to replicate quickly.

**Technical differentiation in specialised segments.** Pharmaceutical and medical packaging, high-barrier flexible pouches for perishables, and ultra-lightweight sustainable packaging formats require significant R&D investment and proprietary process know-how. These segments carry higher margins and stickier customer relationships than commodity films.

**Procurement scale.** USD 22bn in revenue creates meaningful purchasing power with resin and aluminium suppliers, giving Amcor a modest cost-of-materials advantage over smaller regional competitors.

**Moat limitations.** The moat is not wide because:
- Raw material pass-through clauses mean Amcor's margin is on the conversion step only, which is competed for aggressively at contract renewal
- Private-label competition in commodity packaging (shrink wrap, standard polyethylene bags) is intense
- Substitute materials (paper, bioplastics) represent a slow-moving but real threat in some segments
- The Berry acquisition added significant commodity rigid packaging volumes that carry lower moat characteristics than Amcor's legacy flexible packaging

**Five-year moat durability test:** The switching cost and scale advantages are durable. The key question is whether the commodity Berry volumes dilute the average quality of the combined business. For now, the answer is only marginally — the Berry deal also brought patented closure technologies, specialty bottles for personal care and food, and exposure to high-growth segments. The narrow moat survives five years with stable/stable confidence.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity:** No material concerns. Pass.
**Capital allocation:** Mixed — generally competent; Berry acquisition is a high-stakes, high-leverage bet that will define the next decade.
**Owner mentality:** Moderate — executive team has long tenure and meaningful stock ownership, though the sheer scale of the Berry deal suggests institutional imperative risk.

**Key observations:**

*CEO succession:* Ron Delia, who led Amcor for over a decade and architected the Berry transaction, stepped down following deal close. Peter Konieczny took over as CEO. Succession risk is real when the deal architect exits, but Konieczny is a long-serving internal executive with deep operational knowledge of the combined business. This is a yellow flag, not a red flag.

*Berry acquisition thesis:* The deal was announced in 2024 and closed mid-2025. The strategic logic — scale, synergies, North American rigid packaging entry — is coherent. The execution risk is the leverage: taking on ~USD 16bn in debt to merge with a similarly-sized company left the combined entity with net debt/EBITDA approaching 5x. This is the highest leverage Amcor has ever carried. Whether management has the discipline to prioritise debt reduction over buybacks and dividend growth will be the critical capital allocation test of the next three years.

*Dividend policy:* Maintaining a 6.1% dividend while GAAP FCF barely covers interest (let alone the payout) is a clear signal that management intends to pay from adjusted/underlying free cash flow. This is defensible IF integration costs are genuinely one-time. It becomes problematic if they are not. I'd prefer management explicitly frame a path to dividend coverage on a GAAP basis.

*Shareholder communications:* The 1-for-5 reverse stock split (completed January 15, 2026) was a sound administrative decision — at ~USD 2 pre-split, the NYSE listing was approaching penny-stock territory, which affects institutional eligibility and index inclusion. This was a cosmetic but necessary corporate action.

*Institutional imperative warning:* The Berry deal was the largest in Amcor's history. When large acquisitions are funded by large debt loads, there is classic institutional imperative pressure to "complete the integration narrative" even when synergies disappoint. Watch management's synergy guidance closely against actual segment margin improvement.

#### Valuation Reconciliation

*The code central fair value of AUD 47.36 diverges from my reconciled midpoint (AUD 67) by 41%. Per the reconciliation protocol, I must explain this gap.*

**Step 1 — Verify inputs for error:**
All authoritative inputs (EPS, shares, price, revenue, debt) match the provided market data exactly. No input error is present. The gap is entirely driven by the normalised earnings assumption.

**Step 2 — Which assumption drives the gap:**
The code normalises earnings as the median of three estimates:
- Forward NI: AUD 2,889m (= AUD 6.25 × 462.3m shares) → **AUD 6.25/share**
- Margin-normalised: AUD 1,752m → **AUD 3.79/share**
- Historical median OE: AUD 1,259m → **AUD 2.72/share**
- **Median chosen: AUD 3.79/share → 3.79 × 12.5 = AUD 47.36**

The historical median OE (AUD 1,259m) reflects pre-Berry Amcor — roughly half the current company's size. Using it in the median drags normalised earnings down to a figure that is no longer representative of the structurally transformed business.

**Step 3 — Adjusted assumption with rationale:**
The Berry acquisition permanently expanded Amcor's revenue base from ~USD 14bn to ~USD 22bn. Using historical OE as a median component is anchoring to a company that no longer exists in the same form. A more appropriate normalisation:
- Weight forward NI at 55%: AUD 2,889m × 0.55 = AUD 1,589m
- Weight margin-normalised at 45%: AUD 1,752m × 0.45 = AUD 788m
- **Adjusted normalised earnings: AUD 2,377m = AUD 5.14/share**
