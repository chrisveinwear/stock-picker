# Amcor plc (ASX: AMC) — Investment Research Report

**VERDICT: Watch** — This is a world-class packaging franchise hiding behind a highly leveraged balance sheet; the business I would love to own at a price I would love to pay, except that the Berry acquisition has strapped $14 billion of debt across what was once a temple of capital discipline.

**Price:** AUD $17.50 (post 1-for-5 reverse split; equivalent USD ~$11.03) | **IV Range:** AUD $20–$38 | **Margin of Safety:** ~40% to midpoint IV of AUD $29 | **Dividend Yield:** ~7.5%

*Note: Amcor reports in USD and is dual-listed on ASX (AMC) and NYSE (AMCR). All USD figures converted at AUD/USD 0.63 unless stated. Shares outstanding ~485M post-split (1-for-5 reverse split completed January 15, 2026). FY2026E = July 2025 – June 2026.*

## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

---

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside** — firmly and comfortably.

Amcor makes the packaging that surrounds almost every consumer product you buy. Its two divisions are Flexibles (pouches, films, bags, laminates — ~62% of revenue) and Rigid Packaging (plastic bottles, jars, closures — ~38%, expanded by the Berry Global acquisition). The business makes money by converting raw materials (polymer resins, aluminium foil, paper) into precision-engineered packaging solutions under multi-year supply agreements with Unilever, Nestlé, P&G, Kraft Heinz, GSK, and similar consumer staples and healthcare giants. Customers specify Amcor's packaging during product development; switching to a competitor involves requalifying production lines, retesting shelf-life and barrier properties, and regaining regulatory clearances — a 12–18 month process that rarely pencils out economically. Revenue is structurally recurring: consumers open and discard packages every day, customers need replacements in perpetuity, and Amcor is one of a handful of global suppliers with the scale and technical depth to serve a multinational client's full geographic footprint from a single relationship. I can explain this business to a ten-year-old and to a hedge fund analyst using the same one paragraph, and that is exactly what Buffett demands.

---

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

These are the load-bearing beams of any bull thesis on AMC. Check each one quarterly.

1. **Berry integration delivers ~$530M in targeted synergies** (manufacturing consolidation, procurement leverage, overlapping SG&A reduction) on the announced 3-year timeline. As at FY2026, roughly $120–150M run-rate has been captured; the remainder is not yet in the P&L.

2. **Net debt declines from ~$14B toward ~$11B by FY2028** via free cash flow conversion, keeping the company investment-grade and avoiding refinancing stress. Current FCF of ~$830M p.a. is modest relative to the debt load; synergy-driven EBITDA expansion is the primary lever.

3. **Consumer volume destocking has fully cleared**, and Flexibles volumes return to low single-digit organic growth consistent with underlying consumer staples demand. Q2 FY2026 revenue growth of 68% (largely Berry) masked subdued organic volumes; Q3/Q4 must confirm the restocking cycle is complete.

4. **ESG-driven substitution away from plastics remains gradual** and does not accelerate beyond current rates. Amcor's "AmLite" and recyclable mono-material innovations give it a defensible sustainability roadmap; a step-change regulatory or consumer shift toward paper/glass would impair the Flexibles moat faster than anticipated.

5. **AUD/USD stays broadly in the 0.60–0.68 range**, preserving economic parity for ASX investors. AMC generates USD earnings paid as USD dividends; a materially stronger AUD would reduce AUD-denominated returns.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Switching costs (primary) + scale/cost advantage (secondary)
**Strength:** Narrow-to-wide | **Trend:** Stable, with ESG headwind risk on the margin

**The switching cost moat is real and deep.** Packaging is not a commodity when you are a multinational CPG company. A Unilever pouch for a haircare product must meet precise barrier specifications, print fidelity requirements, run at specified line speeds on existing filling equipment, and carry product shelf-life validations that took 12 months to obtain. When Amcor supplies that pouch from 15 different plants across 40 countries under a single global contract, it is not easily displaced by a regional competitor offering 5% lower pricing. The total cost of switching — line downtime, requalification, regulatory re-registration, supply chain reconfiguration — typically runs to millions of dollars per SKU. Amcor serves tens of thousands of SKUs. Customers do not leave; the economics of loyalty are compelling.

**The scale advantage reinforces the moat.** With 500+ manufacturing facilities on every inhabited continent, Amcor can follow customers as they expand into emerging markets without disrupting established supply chains. A smaller regional competitor cannot match this infrastructure; it would take decades and billions of capital to replicate. Raw material purchasing scale (buying billions of kilograms of resin annually) generates procurement terms that a mid-size rival cannot access. The Berry acquisition, whatever its leverage sins, expanded this geographic and product footprint materially: Berry's rigid packaging capabilities in North America — previously a relative gap for Amcor — now make Amcor's offering fully comprehensive.

**The 10-year moat durability test.** The underlying need — mass-produced consumer goods requiring high-performance protective packaging — is not going away. However, two structural risks could narrow the moat over a decade: (1) ESG regulation that mandates specific materials (paper, glass) or bans flexible plastic in certain applications, forcing customers to restructure supply chains regardless of switching cost economics; and (2) consolidation among CPG customers reducing Amcor's relative bargaining power and increasing contract size (and thus, renegotiation leverage for the buyer). Both risks are real but gradual. Amcor's investment in recyclable packaging technology (over $2B in R&D and equipment since 2019) partially offsets the first risk. Verdict: moat is likely stable over 10 years, narrowing risk is present but not imminent.

**Franchise vs. Commodity test.** If Amcor raised packaging prices 5–8%, would customers leave en masse? No — for the reasons above. They would negotiate hard at contract renewal, and they would extract concessions, but they would not disrupt their own supply chains. This is franchise-adjacent behaviour, not commodity behaviour. The proof: Amcor's gross margins have held at 21–23% across cycles, which would not be possible in a purely commodity business.

**Goodwill note.** Post-Berry, Amcor carries ~$19B of goodwill and acquisition intangibles on its balance sheet. This inflates invested capital (artificially depressing ROIC) and generates ~$500M/year in non-cash amortisation charges that depress reported earnings. Stripping this back to the underlying operating business, tangible ROIC on pre-existing manufacturing assets runs 15–18% — a figure consistent with a business that genuinely creates economic value.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity: Pass** — No accounting fraud, no material self-dealing, proactive communication on Berry integration progress.

**Capital allocation: Needs scrutiny.** The Berry Global acquisition (closed November 2024) is the defining capital allocation decision of recent Amcor management. The industrial logic is sound: Berry's complementary North American rigid packaging portfolio, combined with Amcor's Flexibles strength and Asia-Pacific scale, creates a more comprehensive global offering with substantial procurement synergies. The execution so far — integration teams on schedule, first synergies being captured, Q2 FY2026 guidance reaffirmed — is encouraging. However, the deal introduced $14B of combined net debt onto a balance sheet that previously carried $6.7B. Net debt/EBITDA of ~4.2x is well above Amcor's historical comfort zone of ~2.5x and Buffett's preferred ceiling of 2x. This is precisely the "institutional imperative" Buffett warns about: the organisational pressure to grow through acquisition when organic growth is modest. Amcor's track record before Berry — consistent dividend growth, disciplined bolt-on acquisitions, >15% pre-goodwill ROIC — was exemplary. Berry is the first deal large enough to genuinely test that discipline. Management's stated priority of rapid deleveraging to <3x Net Debt/EBITDA by FY2028 must be held accountable with quarterly rigour.

**Owner mentality: Present but diluted.** CEO Peter Konieczny (previously Amcor's Chief Strategy Officer) was elevated to lead the integration phase; the June 2026 leadership appointments signal an acceleration from integration into growth mode. Management compensation is tied to ROIC improvement and deleveraging milestones — appropriate incentive alignment. However, the Berry deal was structured and signed under prior CEO Ron Delia, whose tenure saw the company move toward the boundary of conservative capital management. New leadership inheriting a leveraged balance sheet will be judged on how quickly they restore the pre-Berry financial discipline.

**Institutional imperative risk: Moderate.** The Berry acquisition shows signs of the imperative — a very large deal that doubled company size, introduced complexity, and required significant optimism on synergy delivery. That said, Amcor had been openly discussing the need for scale in the North American rigid market for years, and Berry was a logical partner rather than a diversifying empire-building move. The imperative risk is elevated but not disqualifying.

### 7. Sell Criteria Check

**1. Price severely overvalued?** No — at AUD $17.50 this is significantly below any reasonable estimate of intrinsic value. The stock would need to reach AUD $31–35+ before triggering a valuation-based review.

**2. Fundamental moat destruction?** Not at present. Switching costs remain intact; no major customer defections; the Berry integration, though complex, has not impaired the core flexible packaging franchise. The ESG structural risk — regulatory bans on flexible plastics — bears watching closely and warrants an upgrade in monitoring frequency. If the EU moves to ban flexible plastic food contact applications at a timeline materially faster than 2035–2040, this moat assessment requires immediate revision.

**3. Management integrity issue?** No evidence of any. Disclosures are transparent; the reverse split was communicated well in advance; Q2 FY2026 results and guidance were straightforward. The June 2026 leadership appointments appear to signal operational intent rather than management flight. Pass.

**4. Significantly better opportunity?** Possibly — the ASX contains several businesses with comparable quality characteristics (WTC, XRO, CSL, BXB) at lower leverage and clearer earnings trajectories. If a portfolio already holds a high-conviction, less-leveraged name, adding AMC before clearer synergy confirmation may not represent the highest-conviction use of capital. For a fresh dollar, AMC is competitive at current prices; as a displacement trade it requires more certainty.

### 13. Bridgewater Risk Assessment

**Interest rate sensitivity: Moderate-to-High.** Amcor carries ~$14B net debt, the majority at fixed rates but with ~30% floating or due for refinancing within 3 years. A 100bps rise in credit spreads (e.g., if Amcor loses investment-grade rating) would add ~$42M/year in interest costs. A 200bps spike across the whole portfolio would add ~$280M — erasing approximately 20% of EBITDA. The current RBA and Federal Reserve cutting cycle is supportive.

**Inflation sensitivity: Moderate (net).** Amcor passes through raw material cost increases (resin, aluminium) via contractual price adjustment mechanisms, typically with a 1–2 quarter lag. Sustained high inflation can create short-term margin compression between purchase and pass-through. Labour inflation in manufacturing has been a persistent FY2025–2026 headwind; synergy savings from plant consolidations partially offset this.

**Recession stress test:**
- Estimated drawdown in a GFC-style recession: 40–55% from current levels (leveraged equity is highly cyclical at the margin)
- EBITDA impact: packaging demand for consumer staples is defensive; EBITDA likely falls only 8–12% in a mild recession

### 17. McKinsey Macro Context (Australia)

**RBA rate outlook and impact:**
The RBA has been in a cautious easing cycle through 2025–2026, with the cash rate having declined from a 4.35% peak to approximately 3.75–4.00% by June 2026. This is marginally positive for AMC through two channels: (1) a lower AUD/USD (typically, RBA cuts weaken AUD relative to the USD trend, improving the AUD value of USD earnings for Australian investors in AMC); and (2) reduced domestic discount rates inflate the DCF value of long-duration earnings streams. The direct impact on AMC's USD borrowing costs is minimal — its debt is USD-denominated and tied to US credit markets.

**Australian inflation and GDP trends:**
Australian CPI is tracking back toward the 2.5–3.0% RBA target range. Consumer spending, while constrained by the mortgage refinancing drag through 2024–2025, is recovering. This supports a gradual recovery in Amcor's packaging volumes for Australian and Asia-Pacific customers — but this represents less than 12% of Amcor's total revenue, so the macro tailwind is modest.

**AUD strength impact:**
AUD/USD at 0.63 is below the historical medium-term average of ~0.72. For Australian holders of AMC (which earns and pays dividends in USD): a weaker AUD is beneficial — a 10% AUD depreciation increases AUD-denominated DPS by 10%. The current AUD level is supportive. If AUD were to appreciate to 0.72+ on a commodity/China recovery cycle, this would reduce AUD-equivalent returns by ~14%.

**Sector rotation signals:**
Global equity markets have been rotating out of high-growth/high-multiple technology into dividend-paying, defensive industrial names — a tailwind for AMC's yield profile. Packaging sector valuation multiples globally appear to be troughing after the 2023–2025 de-rating driven by volume declines and leverage concerns. If this rotation continues, re-rating of EV/EBITDA multiples from 5.8× toward 8× would add substantial value.

**Specific macro tailwinds for Amcor:**
1. China economic recovery driving packaging demand across food and healthcare in Asia-Pacific (new CNAS accreditation opens a faster commercial pathway)
2. US consumer staples volumes stabilising after destocking cycle, supporting Berry's North American rigid packaging business
3. Healthcare packaging growth — pharma and medical device packaging continues to outgrow general consumer packaging; Amcor's healthcare exposure (~15% of Flexibles revenue) is a structural tailwind

**Specific macro headwinds:**
1. European consumer weakness — AMC derives ~25% of revenue from Europe; sluggish EU GDP and consumer confidence are compressing volumes
2. Global resin price volatility — input costs can spike rapidly if oil/gas prices surge, creating margin compression before contract pass-through mechanisms activate
3. ESG regulatory headwind — the UK Plastic Packaging Tax, EU packaging regulation (PPWR), and similar frameworks across developed markets create ongoing compliance costs and eventual product substitution risk

**Timeline for macro factors to affect AMC:**
- Volume recovery: H2 FY2026 / FY2027 — the destocking normalisation should be confirmed in the August 2026 full-year result
- ESG regulation impact: Gradual 5–10 year horizon; no near-term step-change
- China revenue benefit (from CNAS): FY2027–FY2028 as regulatory approval converts to signed supply agreements
- RBA easing effect: Already partially priced in; marginal benefit through AUD weakness in FY2026
