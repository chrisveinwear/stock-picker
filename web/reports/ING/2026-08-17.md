## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Inside the boundary, but only just.** Inghams breeds, hatches, grows, processes and distributes chicken (and a smaller turkey business) across Australia and New Zealand, selling into major retail (Coles, Woolworths), quick-service restaurants (Inghams is a longstanding supplier to KFC in Australia) and foodservice channels. There is nothing exotic here — I can explain the whole value chain to a ten-year-old. The complication isn't the business model, it's the economics: protein processing is capital-intensive, margins are structurally thin (gross margin 17.4%, operating margin 3.9%, net margin just 1.8%), and the customer base is concentrated among a handful of powerful retail buyers who set the terms. This is the same trap I fell into with the Berkshire textile mill — an easy-to-understand business that is nonetheless a poor economic animal. I'll analyse it, but I go in with my eyes open about what kind of business this actually is.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

1. Chicken remains the cheapest mainstream animal protein in Australia/NZ, supporting stable-to-growing per-capita volumes through the cycle.
2. The Inghams/Baiada duopoly structure holds — biosecurity licensing, integrated hatchery-to-retail supply chains and entrenched retail relationships keep new entrants out.
3. Feed input costs (grain, soy) and energy costs stabilise rather than structurally re-rate higher, letting operating margin recover from the current 3.9% toward a more normal ~5%+.
4. Coles, Woolworths and QSR partners (including KFC) renew major supply agreements on broadly similar terms rather than squeezing further via private-label expansion or aggressive re-tendering.
5. The elevated capex cycle (capex ran from -$62m in FY22 to -$162m in FY24, well above depreciation) is close to its peak, freeing up free cash flow and dividend capacity from FY27 onward.

If any of 3–5 breaks, the thesis breaks with it — these are the things to watch every half.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Cost/scale advantage from vertical integration, reinforced by a mild switching-cost moat on the retail-contract side (category management arrangements are sticky once embedded).
**Strength:** Narrow at best. Morningstar rates this business **"No Moat"** and the numbers back them up — more on that below.
**Trend:** Narrowing. Two consecutive years of earnings disappointments (an earnings miss and multi-year-low share price in September 2025, followed by a dividend cut alongside weak H1 FY26 results in February 2026) point to eroding pricing power against feed-cost inflation and retail bargaining power, not a temporary blip.

**Five-year moat durability test:** Would I bet Inghams still earns superior returns on capital in 2031? I'm not confident. Do the DuPont math: ROE is a headline-flattering **22.8%**, but that is almost entirely a leverage artefact. Back out invested capital (debt + implied equity − cash ≈ AUD 1.68bn) against EBIT (3.9% operating margin × $3.15bn revenue ≈ $123m), and after-tax ROIC comes out around **5%** — below what I'd want as a cost of capital for a business with this much financial risk. A "wide moat" business doesn't need 5.85x debt-to-equity to generate a respectable-looking ROE. This is a commodity-adjacent processor with a structural cost position, not a business with pricing power.

### 4. Management Assessment

No integrity red flags in the data provided — I have no evidence of self-dealing, accounting games, or promises broken to shareholders beyond the ordinary disappointment of a guidance cut. But there is a genuine **execution flag**: the company heads into its FY26 results on 21 August 2026 with a **new CEO and an interim CFO** at the table (per the results-date announcement). Leadership transitions at the moment a business is under margin pressure are exactly when institutional-imperative risk (chasing growth or empire-building to look busy, rather than fixing the base business) tends to show up. I'd want to hear the new CEO's capital-allocation priorities directly before giving management the benefit of the doubt on the next leg of capex.

Capital allocation track record over the visible window (FY22–24) is mixed-to-decent: net debt fell from ~$1.67bn to ~$1.42bn today, and book equity grew from $158m to $220m+ — a real, multi-year deleveraging trend that gets lost if you only look at the current 5.85x D/E snapshot. That's a point in management's favour. Against it: the dividend was cut in February 2026, which — while often the right call — indicates the board didn't have the cushion it wanted.

**Watch item:** A "change in substantial holding" filing from MUFG hit the ASX on 13 August 2026, four days before this report. The headline doesn't specify direction (increase or decrease), and I won't guess — flag it for the next filing review.
