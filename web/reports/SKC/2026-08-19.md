## PART A — BUFFETT VALUE FRAMEWORK

### 1. Circle of Competence

**Boundary.** The operating business itself is simple to describe: SkyCity runs licensed casinos and integrated resorts in Auckland, Hamilton, Queenstown (NZ) and Adelaide (Australia) — it collects a rake on gaming, hotel, and F&B spend under government-granted licences. That part anyone can understand.

What pushes it to the *boundary* rather than comfortably inside the circle is that gaming/casinos is **not** one of this mandate's approved sectors (consumer staples, financials, healthcare, infrastructure, tech, selective resources). Casinos combine three traits Buffett has historically been wary of: (1) heavy fixed-cost, high-capex infrastructure with thin returns on capital (ROE here is 2.4%), (2) revenue tied to a single government licence that can be revoked, fined, or re-taxed at a regulator's discretion, and (3) a track record — sector-wide, not just at SkyCity — of anti-money-laundering (AML) and social-harm compliance failures that have destroyed shareholder value overnight (Star Entertainment's near-death experience is the reference case explicitly raised in the news flow on this stock). SkyCity itself has an AML compliance history in both Auckland and Adelaide that regulators continue to scrutinise.

This isn't disqualifying on its own — Buffett has owned regulated, capital-intensive businesses (utilities, railroads) where the moat is the licence itself. But it does mean the analysis below should be read with a wider error bar than a staple or a toll road, and management/regulatory-integrity questions (Section 4) carry more weight than usual in the final verdict.

### 2. Key Assumptions (3–5)

1. **No further AML/regulatory shock.** The valuation assumes no material new fine, licence suspension, or forced governance overhaul beyond what's already reflected in the depressed earnings base (a genuine tail risk given the sector's recent history — see Section 13).
2. **Debt reduction via asset sales continues.** The Auckland property sale (flagged in July 2026 news as advancing) is assumed to proceed and materially reduce the NZ$803m debt load; if it stalls, leverage risk re-rates the stock down, not up.
3. **Earnings trough is behind or near-complete.** Owner earnings are normalised off a forward-analyst estimate (~AUD 36.2m) rather than the FY2024/25 trough — this assumes FY26 (reported tomorrow) and FY27 show recovering, not further-deteriorating, margins after the H1 FY26 67% profit plunge.
4. **No dividend resumption priced in.** Yield is n/a; the model assumes retained cash is used for deleveraging, not distributions, over the explicit forecast horizon.
5. **Capex normalises lower.** FY2023–25 capex ran NZ$164–311m/year (Auckland NZICC-related build-out); the model assumes this falls toward a maintenance level as major projects complete, which is what allows owner earnings to turn positive in the model's out-years.

### 3. Business Quality & Moat

**Moat type:** Regulatory/licence-based (a limited number of casino licences exist in each NZ/SA jurisdiction) with some scale advantage in integrated-resort format (hotel + convention + gaming + F&B bundled).

**Strength: none-to-narrow.** Morningstar's independent rating agrees — **Economic Moat: None** (see Morningstar cross-check below). A licence monopoly is only a moat if it converts into pricing power and superior returns on capital; here it hasn't: ROE is 2.4%, net margin 4.3%, and the business has burned cash (FCF −NZ$107m to −NZ$119m) in three of the last four years. A true moat business doesn't need to sell property to fund its balance sheet.

**Trend: narrowing.** Gross margin held reasonably (47.9%), but operating margin (5.4%) and net margin (4.3%) show a business where fixed costs (compliance, security, facilities) are eating most of the gross profit. Equity has shrunk from NZ$1,571m (FY2022) to NZ$1,330m (FY2025) — a 15% erosion over three years despite two of those years being net-income-positive, which tells you capex has been consuming more value than it created.

**Five-year moat durability test:** Would SkyCity still command customers, pricing, and licences in 2031? Probably operationally yes — casino licences aren't easily replicated and NZ/SA aren't adding competing venues. But "durable" and "valuable" are different questions; a durable licence attached to persistently sub-cost-of-capital returns is not a moat in the Buffett sense, it's a regulated utility without the utility's return guarantee.

### 4. Management Assessment

**Integrity:** No auto-veto trigger identified in the supplied data, but this is the section to watch most closely given the sector's history. The AustralianSuper stake reduction (voting power easing to 8.32%, per recent news) is a data point worth monitoring but not itself a red flag — large holders trim for many reasons.

**Capital allocation track record:** Mixed-to-poor over the observable window. NZ$650–800m of debt has funded a multi-year capex programme (Auckland NZICC, property development) that has so far produced negative free cash flow in 3 of 4 years and a falling equity base. The Auckland property sale to accelerate deleveraging is the right instinct — but it's a **defensive** capital-allocation move (shoring up the balance sheet), not an offensive one (reinvesting into a widening moat), and it's happening because the balance sheet needs it, not from a position of strength.

**Owner mentality / institutional imperative:** The heavy capex cycle through a period of weak returns has the hallmarks of "we're building it because we started building it" institutional-imperative risk rather than disciplined, return-on-capital-driven investment. No dividend currently — appropriate given the balance sheet, but it also means shareholders have had no cash return to show for a decade of capital deployment.

**Verdict on management:** Not disqualifying, but this is not "wonderful management at a fair price" territory. Watch the FY26 result (due tomorrow) for tone on capital discipline and the AML/compliance narrative specifically.
